# Technique

What the plan's steps rely on, with the reasons, for answering builders' questions.

## Milling
- Mill all blanks of one bar shape in one session at one planer setting. A 1/64" thickness
  difference between rhombi shows as a step at every glue line.
- Quartersawn or rift stock moves less and shows tight rings in end grain; flatsawn is fine for
  rectangular bars.
- Joint one edge before the first bevel rip; the first bevelled strip is waste.

## Bevel ripping (rhombi, parallelograms, triangles)
- Set the tilt with a digital angle gauge on the blade body (not the teeth), then cut a test bar and
  check two of them against each other: put them together into the angle they should make
  (two 60-degree rhombi make 120; three make a hexagon) and look for light.
- Parallelograms: rip every bar off the same bevelled edge; each pass cuts the next bar's edge, so no
  flipping. Width at the table equals the face side.
- Triangles: flip the blank face for face before each pass so the bevels alternate.
- Keep the bar on the free side of the blade (a thin-rip jig in the left miter slot, fence moved
  each pass), so it is not trapped between a tilted blade and the fence. Use a zero-clearance insert
  for small bars, a push block that holds the blank down, and a featherboard.
- Rip 10% spares: bevel bars chip and bow.

## Units (tape glue-ups)
- Lay bars face down edge to edge on packing tape, in order; glue the bevels; fold up so the tape
  closes the joints; wrap with tape or rubber bands. Check against a printed template while wet.
- Titebond III (longer open time) for many-joint units; Titebond II is fine for strips.

## Logs
- Glue on a flat surface with waxed paper or packing tape on cauls. Clamp pressure perpendicular to
  each glue line; cauls above and below keep bars in plane.
- Logs with zigzag faces (rows of hexagons) are self-locating; they need gentle side pressure too.
- Scrape squeeze-out before crosscutting so the log sits flat.

## Crosscutting slices
- Crosscut sled with a stop block; slice thickness = finished + 1/8".
- Draw a pencil V across the log before cutting: it keeps slice order and which face is up.
- A log that can rock (no two flat adjacent faces) goes on a plywood carrier with double-sided tape.
- Never trap a slice between blade and stop block: the stop block sits before the blade (clamped to
  the sled fence ahead of the cut) or the off-cut side is held down.

## Gluing the board
- End grain wicks glue: size both faces with a thin coat, wait two minutes, recoat, then clamp.
- Glue in stages of 2 to 4 slices; then join the stages. Light pressure, cauls top and bottom so the
  faces stay flush (they are end grain and slow to flatten).

## Flattening and finishing
- Router sled with a 1" or larger surfacing bit, or a drum sander with light passes. Not a thickness
  planer: end grain tears out at the trailing edge and pieces can be thrown. If a planer is the only
  option, glue sacrificial long-grain strips to both ends, chamfer the trailing edge, and take 1/64"
  passes on a sharp helical head; it is still a risk.
- Sand 80, 120, 180, raise the grain with a damp cloth, 220.
- Mineral oil flood coats until it stops soaking in (end grain takes several over two days), then
  board butter (mineral oil + beeswax). No film finishes on a cutting surface.

## Options
- Edges: 1/8" chamfer or round-over on every edge; end-grain corners chip if left sharp.
- Feet: rubber feet with stainless screws, 1" in from the corners; the board dries evenly underneath.
- Juice groove: 1/2" core-box bit, 3/4" from the edge, two passes; end grain burns if the bit lingers.
- Handles: cove or core-box cuts in the ends on the router table.
