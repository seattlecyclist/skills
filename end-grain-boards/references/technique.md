# Technique

How patterned end-grain boards are built, and why the plan says what it says. Read before writing
advice the plan does not already give.

## The rules everything follows

1. **Machine every glue-up flat before the next one.** A face that goes into a joint has been planed,
   jointed or sanded since the last time glue touched it. This is why the board is built from small
   closed logs (triangles and rectangles): every face of them can be machined. Never design a
   glue-up whose outside is a zigzag or a V-groove; it cannot be cleaned and the next joint gaps.
2. **Few parts per glue-up.** Three or four bars into a log, two slices into a pair, pairs into a
   row, two or three rows at a time. Only square-edged strips (a checkerboard panel) go together
   many at once.
3. **One setting per dimension.** Every bar of a thickness through the planer in one session; every
   log through the sander at one setting. Parts that match each other matter more than parts that
   match the drawing.
4. **Rip over, machine to size.** The saw gets a bar within 1/32"; the planer or sanding sled makes
   it right.
5. **Test on scrap.** Three short bars dry-fitted in the cradle show a wrong tilt before good wood
   is cut.

## Milling

Acclimate a week. Joint a face and an edge, plane to the thickness in the material list. Straight
grain, quartersawn or rift where possible.

## Ripping bars

Blade tilt set with a digital angle gauge. For any tilted rip, the bar comes off on the open side of
the blade against a thin-rip stop; the fence moves in for each cut. Never trap a bar between a
tilted blade and the fence. Stop when the blank is 1" wide; that strip is waste. Zero-clearance
insert, push block, featherboard ahead of the blade.

- Diamonds and parallelograms: bevel the blank's edge, then rip bar after bar without flipping.
  The two sawn faces are parallel, so the bar lies on one and goes through the planer or sander to
  clean the other.
- Triangles and trapezoids: flip the blank face for face before every pass. True the sawn faces in
  the sanding sled.

## Triangle logs (60 degree patterns)

Glue in a V cradle: two MDF strips with 30 degree edges on a flat base, lined with packing tape.
Bars stack in flat layers, tip down; a taped caul on the flat top and clamps every 4 to 5 inches.
The V closes the joints sideways as the caul pushes down. A log that needs a second layer gets its
top sanded flat first. True all three faces in a sanding sled (the same V, no tape) through a drum
sander, or with a jack plane, taking about 1/64" and the same passes on every log.

Four trued logs glue into a log of twice the side the same way (`levels` 2): fewer, larger slices.

## Square logs (45 degree patterns)

Bands are glued flat on tape between cauls and planed. Quarter logs are glued in corner cauls (two
L-shaped cauls) so clamps work from two directions. Two joints at right angles are never glued at
once: quarters into halves, halves into blocks, each jointed and planed square in between.

## Crosscutting slices

Mark a crayon stripe down the log's reference face (the caul face) so every slice carries it.
Crosscut sled, stop block, hold-down on the fence side; the slice is free on the stop side, so stop
the saw before reaching for it. A 10" saw clears about 3-1/8": a log on a 1/2" sled base can be
2-5/8" tall. Slices are cut 1/8" over finished thickness.

## Rows, then the board

Triangular slices glue in pairs into diamonds, pairs into a row between the two fences of a row
jig, with 60 degree end blocks (a waste slice cut in half) so the clamps bear on square faces.
The faces being glued are the trued sides of the logs: long grain, one thin coat each side, moderate
pressure. Do not size these faces as if they were end grain.

Check each row's edges with a straightedge; skim on the table saw taped to a straight carrier, or
on a sanding board. Never the jointer or planer: the knives meet the fibres end-on.

Rows glue two or three at a time with cauls across both faces and a stop at one end. Register by
the slice corners, which meet corner to corner, and pencil lines drawn across the dry fit.

## Flatten, then trim

Drum sander or router sled; never a thickness planer on end grain. Flatten first, then trim with a
backer and tape on the cut line, so trimming removes any snipe and chipped edges. Sand 80 to 220,
raising the grain before the last grit.

## Finish and options

Ease edges (1/8" chamfer or round-over). Food-grade mineral oil to refusal, then oil-and-beeswax
board butter. Feet let the underside dry. Juice grooves in two light passes with a core-box bit.

## Wood movement

End-grain boards move across both face directions. Keep shrinkage figures within about 4 points
across the species, borders in end grain only (never a long-grain frame), and tell the owner to oil
both faces.
