Overview

A lattice of thin purpleheart lines on near-black wenge, drawn as six-sided cells, with a small padauk diamond wherever four lines meet
18" × 11-15/16" × 1-1/2"
21 of 21 checks pass

How it goes together

  • The whole board is one square tile, 1-1/2" on a side: a purpleheart strip 3/16" thick crossing a wenge square on a 35 degree lean, with a corner of padauk at each end of the strip.
  • Upright, the strip is steep. On its side, the same tile has a shallow strip. Rows of upright tiles alternate with rows of tiles on their sides, and the steep and shallow zigzags close into six-sided cells, with a diamond of padauk at every corner where four strips meet.
  • What repeats: 1 slab shape and 1 log make everything. Plank A is 12 logs upright, plank B is 12 of the same logs on their sides; each plank is crosscut 4 times. No row is turned or flipped.
  • Nothing is assembled from small pieces. Blanks, slabs, logs and planks are all long, flat-sided and planed between glue-ups. The padauk diamonds are long square sticks glued into V-grooves in the planks, so each one is made of four triangles that are never handled as parts.
  • The base board has no border, like the maker’s: the pattern runs to the edge.

Board feet include 25% for defects and milling. This build is hard on the wenge: about 31% of the wenge in the blanks ends up in logs. The rest is the triangle wasted by the first tilted cut, the 1-1/2" left to keep each blank flat on the saw, and the two sloping edges ripped off every slab. That is the price of never handling a small or bevelled part. The offcuts from squaring are long three-ply strips, worth keeping for another board.

The woods

  • Wenge · Janka 1630 · shrinkage 9.0%. Near black, but splinters and the splinters fester: avoid for working surfaces or use sparingly.
  • Purpleheart · Janka 2520 · shrinkage 6.4%. Vivid purple that browns with UV; very hard on tools.
  • African padauk · Janka 1970 · shrinkage 5.2%. Orange-red; dust is an irritant, and it can bleed red into maple: wipe with solvent before glue.

The maker’s stills show wenge for the ground. The purple is almost certainly purpleheart and the orange looks like padauk; those two are my reading of the colours, not something the maker states where I could read it. Wenge is hard and nearly black, which is what makes the purple show, but its splinters are nasty and its end grain is open-pored. Black walnut is the kinder and cheaper swap ("species": {"ground": "walnut"}); the purple will stand out less against it. Purpleheart and padauk both dull with light over the years: purple toward brown, orange toward red-brown.

The pattern

Read from the maker’s cover photo, then simplified
tile 1-1/2" · strip 3/16" · lean 35°

What the photos show

  • Squared up, the maker’s cover photo shows the stars on a square grid, on every other grid corner like the black squares of a checkerboard. So the board is square tiles, not hexagons.
  • Purple strips join the stars in zigzags that run across the board. Rows of steep zigzags alternate with rows of shallow ones, the steep ones about 35 degrees off upright and the shallow ones about 35 degrees off level. A steep row and a shallow row together close the six-sided cells.
  • A steep tile and a shallow tile are the same tile a quarter turn apart. That is the whole trick, and it is why one log can make the board.
  • A still from the build shows a row of about eight dark blocks, each with one leaning purple strip, alternately leaning left and right, with small pale pieces where the strips meet.

Where this plan departs from the maker’s board

  • His stars are small hourglass shapes boxed in by short purple pieces. Mine are plain padauk diamonds, 3/4" across, that the strips run straight into. The lattice reads the same; the stars are simpler.
  • His dark ground is pieced from more than one tone, in bars and stems that show in the photo. Mine is one wood.
  • His board looks like about 12 tiles by 8. This plan is 12 by 8 at 1-1/2" a tile.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
14 steps · 5 glue-ups
  1. 1

    Mill the plies

    • Joint and plane 6 wenge boards to 1" thick and 3 purpleheart boards to 3/16" thick, all 9-1/4" wide and 30" long. Plane each wood in one session at one setting.
    • The purpleheart thickness is the width of every line in the pattern, so it is the one that shows: measure it with calipers, within 0.005" board to board. The wenge only has to be at least 1".
    • For the purpleheart, resaw a 4/4 board in two on the bandsaw and plane both halves to 3/16" on a flat carrier board. No 9-1/4" boards? Edge-glue narrower ones first; a seam inside a ply shows only as an ordinary glue line in one wood.
  2. 2
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the three-ply blanks

    • Glue wenge, purpleheart, wenge face to face: 3 blanks, each 2-3/16" thick. Roll glue on both faces of the purpleheart, rub the boards together, and clamp between flat cauls with a clamp every 4 to 5 inches. Thin purpleheart cups as soon as it is wet, so have the clamps set before the glue goes on.
    • Next day scrape the squeeze-out, joint one edge of each blank square to its faces and rip the other edge parallel. The jointed edge rides the fence in the next step.
  3. 3

    Rip the slabs with the blade at 35 degrees

    • Tilt the blade to 35 degrees from upright with a digital gauge, leaning away from the fence, and raise it to cut through the 2-3/16" blank (the blade is in 2.67" of wood, close to the limit of a 10 in. saw: use a sharp rip blade and feed steadily). Riving knife on, featherboard ahead of the blade, push blocks.
    • The blank lies flat on the table with its jointed edge against the fence for every cut. The slab is the piece that comes off on the far side of the blade.
    • First cut. Set the fence so the blade just takes the top corner off the far edge. That waste is a triangle.
    • Then move the fence 2-1/16" (2.060) toward the blade for each slab. Each slab comes off about 1-9/16" thick, with the purpleheart crossing it on a lean. Check the first with calipers and adjust the move.
    • Take 3 slabs from each blank and stop: the last 1-1/2" of the blank is what holds it flat. 9 slabs in all; 8 are needed and 1 is for setting up step 5.
    DON'Trip the last slab from a blank that no longer sits flat with its edge on the fence.
  4. 4

    Plane the slabs

    • A slab lies flat on either sawn face, so it goes through the planer like any board. Plane every slab to exactly 1-1/2", taking the same from each face, all at the last setting in one session.
    • That 1-1/2" is one side of the square log. The next step rips the other two sides to the same size.
  5. 5

    Rip each slab square, twice

    • Blade back to 90 degrees. Each slab has two sloping edges, which are the old faces of the blank. Both come off square, at the right distance from the purpleheart.
    • Cut A. Lay the slab so that one of its two sharp corners is down on the table and against the fence, and set the fence 2-39/64" (2.610) from the blade. The sloping edge on the far side of the blade falls away. Test on a spare slab and measure the wenge between the new square edge and the purpleheart: 7/64" (0.110) on the face that was up, 1-5/32" (1.161) on the face that was down. Nudge the fence until both are right, then run every slab.
    • Cut B. Turn the slab so the new square edge is against the fence and rip it to 1-1/2" wide, or to the planed thickness if that measured a hair different. The log must come out square.
    • After both cuts every stick is 1-1/2" square and reads the same from either face: 7/64" (0.110) of wenge, 15/64" (0.229) of purpleheart, 1-5/32" (1.161) of wenge.
  6. 6

    Cut the sticks into logs

    • Check each stick with calipers: as wide as it is tall within 0.005". Half of the logs are used on their sides, so a stick that is not square makes a plank with steps in it.
    • Crosscut each stick into 3 logs of 9-1/2": 24 logs, 12 for each plank. The spare stick gives 3 more.
    • Mill two scrap strips for each plank, any wood, 3/4" wide, 1-1/2" thick and 9-1/2" long. They go on the outside edges of the planks so the two edge grooves can be routed and filled like all the others.
  7. 7
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue plank A: logs upright

    • Stand 12 logs so the purpleheart rises steeply, and turn every other log end for end so the purpleheart makes a zigzag across the end of the plank: 6 V shapes. A scrap strip goes on each outside edge.
    • Look at the end against the drawing before any glue. In plank A the strips come to 7/32" of each other at the turns of the zigzag.
    • Glue in two halves, 6 logs and one scrap strip each, on flat cauls with cauls above, so each part fits a 12.5" planer. Plane both faces of each part with the lightest pass that cleans them, both parts at the same setting. Then glue the parts edge to edge, faces flush.
    • Keep the ends of the logs flush against a stop so no length is lost.
  8. 8
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue plank B: the same logs on their sides

    • Lay the other 12 logs with a quarter turn, so the purpleheart runs at a shallow slope, and again turn every other log end for end. The zigzag is now low and wide, and its peaks and valleys stop 7/32" (0.225) short of the faces.
    • Same glue-up as plank A, with a scrap strip on each outside edge, in two halves, planed to the same thickness as plank A.
    • Both planks must end up the same width, log seam for log seam. Lay one on the other and check before the glue sets on the second.
  9. 9

    Rout the V-grooves

    • A 90 degree V-groove bit, 3/4" or wider, in the router table, set 3/8" high: the groove is 3/4" wide at the face and 3/8" deep. Take it in two passes.
    • The grooves run the length of the plank, each one centred on a glue line. In both planks they go on every other glue line on one face, and on the glue lines between those on the other face. The scrap seams count as glue lines.
    • Plank A. Groove the glue lines where two strips come together at that face. The groove takes away the ends of both strips. Plank B: groove the glue lines where the zigzag comes nearest that face; the groove just reaches its peak.
    • That makes 7 grooves on one face and 6 on the other, 13 per plank. Mark every groove line with a pencil cross on the plank's end first, against the drawings on sheet 05, and stack the planks as they will meet in the board to see that the marks line up.
    • Run the plank flat on the table with a featherboard holding it to the fence, and set the fence from the glue line itself each time, not from a measurement: the point of the bit follows the glue line.
    DON'Tgroove the same glue line on both faces. Every glue line gets one groove, on one face.
  10. 10
    GLUE-UP 4

    Glue in the star sticks

    • Mill 10 padauk sticks 17/32" (0.530) square and 29" long, and cut them to 9-1/2": 26 pieces. Plane them to fit the groove, not to the number: a stick set corner down should fill the groove with its two upper corners level with the face.
    • Wipe padauk with acetone just before glue if it feels oily. Glue a stick corner down in every groove on one face, with half of it standing proud. Clamp through a caul that has the same V-groove routed in it, so the pressure goes straight down on the corner. Do one face, let it cure, then the other.
    • Saw or plane the proud half off: at the planer, 1/32 in. a pass until the sticks are flush, then one cleaning pass. Both planks to the same final thickness.
    • Rip the scrap strips off both edges, splitting the edge sticks down the middle of the glue line. Each plank is now 18" wide and shows half a diamond at each edge groove.
  11. 11

    Crosscut both planks into rows

    • On the crosscut sled with a stop block, square one end of each plank, then cut 4 slices 1-5/8" thick from plank A and 4 from plank B. Each slice is one whole row of the board.
    • Hold the plank down and against the sled fence for every cut. The 2" left at the end is what your hand holds; it is also the spare row if one slice goes wrong, cut last with the plank clamped to the sled.
    • Pencil an arrow on the same edge of every slice and letter them A or B.
  12. 12
    GLUE-UP 5

    Lay out and glue the board

    • Stand the slices on edge, end grain up, all with the same cut face up and every arrow pointing the same way. Alternate them A, B, A, B. No slice is turned or flipped.
    • Check each seam between rows: every half diamond on one slice meets a half diamond on the next, and every purpleheart strip that reaches a seam ends on a diamond. If the diamonds of two slices are a whole log apart, one of them is the wrong way round.
    • Glue the rows with their ends flush against a stop, on taped cauls, clamps across the rows every 4 inches and cauls above to keep them level. These are long-grain faces: one coat of glue, normal pressure.
  13. 13

    Flatten

    • Router sled first, then the random-orbit sander: 80 grit to remove the router marks, then 120, 180, 220. Raise the grain with a damp cloth before the last grit.
    • A drum sander does the same job if you have one. For the helical planer, read sheet 07 before trying it.
    See sheet 07
  14. 14

    Trim, ease the edges, finish

    • Take 1/32" off the two long edges on the table saw, just enough to clean them. The two short edges are the ends of the rows: if they are not dead flush, take the least you can off both on the crosscut sled with a backer, the same from each so the half diamonds stay equal. Finished size 18" x 11-15/16" x 1-1/2".
    • 1/8 in. round-over on every edge. Flood with mineral oil until it stops soaking in, then board butter. Add four rubber feet.
07

Flattening & options

Two ways to flatten without a drum sander, and what can be changed
options

Route 1 · router sled and sander

  • Board on a flat base, shimmed so it cannot rock, between two straight rails; a wide straight or bowl bit in a router on a bridge.
  • Passes of 1/32 in. or less, the full face, then the other face.
  • Sand out the router marks from 80 grit. This is the route the plan assumes and the one every source agrees on.

Route 2 · helical-head planer

  • Up to here the planer has done everything, because every part was long grain. The finished board is end grain, and a planer can tear the trailing edge out or throw the board.
  • If you use it anyway, follow George Vondriska’s method (sheet 08): glue sacrificial long-grain rails to the leading and trailing edges or put a heavy chamfer on the trailing edge, take 1/64 in. or less a pass, sharp inserts, and stand out of line.
  • This plan has not tested it and does not need it. Wenge end grain is brittle and chips at an unsupported edge, so the rails matter more here than on a maple board.

Options

  • Ground wood: walnut in place of wenge is cheaper and kinder to work, with less contrast.
  • Scale: "params": {"tile": 1.25} makes a smaller board from thinner stock; the ply sizes and fence settings are recomputed and the checks say if a strip would miss its star.
  • Border: the maker’s board has none and neither does this plan. If you want one, make it end grain like the field (blocks crosscut from a planed board and stood on end), never a long-grain frame, which cracks the board or itself as the field moves.
  • Juice groove or handles: rout them after flattening; keep a groove clear of the stars at the edge.

Shown by the maker

  • The finished face, in the cover photo of the full video.
  • Wenge as the dark wood, ripped from a thick beam.
  • Dark bars laminated with thin pale strips between them.
  • A glue-up of a row of dark blocks, each with one leaning purple strip, alternately left and right, with small pale pieces at the points where strips meet. That row is a plank in all but name.
  • Stripes of orange and purple running through the thickness at the board’s edges, so the stars are long sticks, not surface inlay.

Shown by other makers

  • Rows crosscut from a glued plank: any checkerboard end-grain board.

Worked out for this plan, not shown

  • I could not play either video here, only read their titles and the still frames YouTube publishes for them. I do not know the order of the maker’s steps between those stills, and his own method is very likely different from mine in the details.
  • The pattern on sheet 03 is my simplified version of the maker’s face, not a copy of it: plain diamonds instead of his boxed hourglass stars, and a one-wood ground. I checked it against the photo by eye, not by measurement.
  • The three-ply blank, its thicknesses, the 35 degree slab and the two squaring rips are my reconstruction, carried over from the two-wood maze plan. The maker’s still suggests he glues leaning strips between separate dark bars instead.
  • Using one log upright for one plank and on its side for the other is my reading of the face. It works on paper for any strip that is the same after half a turn.
  • The stars as square sticks glued corner down into V-grooves routed along the planks’ glue lines, and the scrap strips for the edge grooves, are my method. I have not seen the maker or anyone else do this on a plank. Rout and fill one groove in an offcut first.
  • The helical-planer route for the finished board is Vondriska’s, untested on this board.
  • Nobody has built this plan yet.
