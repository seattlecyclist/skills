Overview

How it goes together

  • Square-edged strips are glued into flat panels, the panels are planed and crosscut, and the slices are stood on end and glued into the board.
  • 11 rows of 8 squares, 1.5" each.

The woods

Bars & rip setups

Every piece of the face is the end of one of these bars. Each drawing shows the section, then the table saw from the front for that rip
16 bars + spares · kerf 1/8"

The bevel rip

The sled. Every bevelled bar is cut on the rip sled (sheet 06, step 2), the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps that the builders in the best practices guide use. It runs against the rip fence, which stays on the side the blade leans away from: its usual place on a left-tilt saw; on a right-tilt saw move the fence to the left of the blade.

Two decks. The fence deck, between the fence and the blade, carries the locating fence and the toggle clamps. The landing deck, beyond the blade and under its lean, catches what comes off. Nothing is ever loose on the saw table.

Trapezoids and diamonds. They start as square-edged strips and are clamped to the fence deck for both bevels; only a waste sliver drops on the landing deck.

After every pass. Push the sled on until the work is clear of the blade, or switch off and wait for the blade to stop, before lifting anything out. Keep the riving knife in place.

The cards below. Each gives what differs for that bar: the strip to start from, how it is turned for the second bevel, and the figure to set the sled to.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
10 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (Hard maple, Black walnut) and let them sit in the shop for a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained stock, quartersawn or rift where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
    See sheet 02
  2. 2

    Make the jigs

    • Flat cauls. Four straight 2x2s with packing tape on one face, as long as the panels are wide.
    • Crosscut sled. With a hold-down clamp, a stop block and a 1" spacer. Check it cuts square in both directions.
  3. 3

    Mill the blanks and rip the bars

    • Joint one face and one edge of every blank, then plane to thickness: 1-1/2" (material list, sheet 02). Set the planer with calipers, not a tape, and plane every blank of one thickness in the same session without moving the planer between them.
    • Crosscut the blanks to the lengths in the cut list. Then rip and true each bar shape as set out on sheet 03, which draws the saw and the rip sled from the front for every bevelled bar. Bars are ripped a little over and then machined to size, so the saw only has to be close.
    • Test each setup on scrap first: rip three short bars, true them, and dry-fit them against a square. Half a degree of bevel error shows as a gap you can slip paper into; fix the tilt before cutting good wood.
    • The cut list has 16 bars plus 2 spares (one in ten, and at least one of every bar in every wood). Rip every bar of a shape in one run, spares included, then check a handful with calipers: all within 1/64".
    See sheet 03
    DON'Trip a bevel with the work loose on the saw table, or with a bar between the tilted blade and the fence. Every bevel is cut with the work clamped to the rip sled.
  4. 4
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the strip panels

    • Log A. Glue 4 x bar 1 (maple), 4 x bar 1 (walnut) as drawn on sheet 04. Make 2 at 14-3/4" long, for 11 slices plus 1 spare.
    • Square-edged strips are the one case where many parts can be glued at once: lay the strips in order on taped cauls, glue the edges, clamp across the panel every 6 inches with cauls above and below to keep it flat.
    • Scrape when rubbery; leave overnight.
    See sheet 04
  5. 5

    True the strip panels

    • Plane both faces of each panel clean and parallel, all panels at one planer setting. These faces are the glue faces of the board. Bring the panels to 1-1/2" thick.
    DON'Tleave a glue face as it came out of the clamps. Dried glue and a part standing a few thousandths proud hold the next joint open.
  6. 6

    Crosscut the slices

    • Square one end of each panel first. Lay the panel against the sled fence and clamp it down.
    • Set the stop block 2-7/8" from the blade and use the 1" spacer: slide the log up to the spacer, clamp the log, take the spacer away, then cut. The slice comes off 1-7/8" thick (finished 1-3/4" plus 1/8" to flatten) with an inch of room beside it, so it cannot bind between blade and stop.
    • Cut 12 slices in all: 11 + 1 spare from log A. Stack them in order.
    • Hold the slice down on the sled base with a push stick as the blade comes through, hand well clear. Push the sled on until the slice is past the blade, stop, and lift the slice away before pulling the sled back.
    • The last 2" of each log is the part the clamp holds. It is never sliced: stop there.
    DON'Tcut a slice against a fixed stop with no spacer, or slice into the last 2 inches of a log. A trapped slice is thrown; a short stub cannot be held.
  7. 7
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the slices into rows

    • The slices are the rows. 11 of them. Lay them out end grain up in the order on sheet 05, turning the ones marked with a prime end for end.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Ttrust a row that rocks in the jig. A slice that sits proud of the fence line will hold the next row off; take it out and find out why.
  8. 8
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue the rows into the board

    • Dry-fit the 11 rows in order and line up the pattern, then draw a pencil line across every joint.
    • Glue two or three rows at a time, then glue those sections together. Clamp across the rows every 4 to 5 inches with moderate pressure, with taped cauls clamped across the top and bottom near each end, inside the ragged ends, to keep the rows in one plane.
    • The faces being glued are long grain (the sides of the logs): one thin rolled coat on one face of each joint. The pencil lines hold the register: check them after the clamps are on and tap a row back if it has crept.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery. Leave the board in clamps overnight and out of clamps a day before flattening.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue every row at once. More than three joints open at a time creep out of plane and out of register before the clamps are on.
  9. 9

    Flatten, then trim

    • Flatten first, trim second. Flatten both faces with a drum sander (60 or 80 grit, light passes) or a router sled (wedge the board in a frame, spoilboard bit, passes of 1/32" or less, outside edges first), then a random-orbit sander; a router sled does the whole job if there is no drum sander. The slices were cut 1/8" thick of finished for this. Stop at 1-3/4".
    • Then trim to 11-3/4" x 16-1/4" with a track saw or on a crosscut sled. The trim lines are centred on the pattern (sheet 05 shows them against the slices): make the left cut 1/8" in from the left edge and the bottom cut 1/8" in from the bottom edge, square to each other, then cut the right and top edges parallel to them at the finished size. That leaves about 1/8" of waste on the right and 1/8" at the top. Tape the cut line and back the exit edge with scrap so the end grain does not chip.
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let it dry, then sand 220.
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain breaks out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  10. 10

    Edges and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1/8" round-over and break the corners: sharp end-grain edges chip.
    • Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners, so the board dries evenly underneath.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
