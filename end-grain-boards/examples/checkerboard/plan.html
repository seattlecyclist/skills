01

Overview

A checkerboard of 1-1/4 inch squares in walnut and maple, nine squares by thirteen, with a walnut square in every corner
11-1/4" × 16-1/4" × 1-3/4"
16 of 16 checks pass

How it goes together

  • Square-edged strips are glued into flat panels, the panels are planed and crosscut, and the slices are stood on end and glued into the board.
  • 13 rows of 9 squares, 1.25" each. Both counts are odd, so all four corners are the same wood and the pattern is the same from either end.
  • The two outside strips of each panel are ripped 1/8" wider than the rest. The trim takes that 1/8" off, so the outside squares finish the same size as the others.

Tools

  • Router sled or drum sander to flatten the board; never a planer on the finished end grain
  • Random-orbit sander, router or block plane for the edges, mineral oil and board butter

The woods

  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood. Softer than maple, so it shows knife marks a little sooner; the usual partner for maple in a checker.
  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.

Bars & rip setups

Every piece of the face is the end of one of these bars. Each drawing shows the section, then the table saw from the front for that rip

1

1-3/8" strip

blade square
  1. Mill the blank to 1-3/8" thick: the panel's thickness plus 1/8" to clean up after glue. Pencil a squiggle along both planed faces before ripping: in the panel those faces lie up and down, and the squiggle tells them from the sawn edges once the strip is nearly square.
  2. Blade square. Rip the strip 1-13/32" wide with the fence 1-13/32" from the blade. Sheet 02 lists what each blank yields, spares included: rip each blank full length, then crosscut every strip to its length in the cut list below.
  3. Stand it on edge and plane the sawn edges to 1-3/8", half off each edge. Take the last pass on every strip of this width, both woods, at one planer setting without touching the handwheel, so the strips of every panel are the same width and the grid lines of one panel meet those of the next. Measure with calipers: this width is a grid line that runs the whole board.

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
10 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (Black walnut, Hard maple) and let them sit in the shop for a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained stock, quartersawn or rift where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
    See sheet 02
  2. 2

    Make the jigs

    • Flat cauls. Eight straight 2x2s (four pairs) with packing tape on one face, about 21" long, so they overhang both the panels and the finished board and the clamps have something to bear on.
    • Crosscut sled. With a hold-down clamp, a stop block and a 1" spacer. Check it cuts square in both directions.
  3. 3

    Mill the blanks and rip the bars

    • Joint one face and one edge of every blank, then plane to thickness: 1-3/8" (material list, sheet 02). Set the planer with calipers, not a tape, and plane every blank of one thickness in the same session without moving the planer between them.
    • Rip each blank full length into the strips sheet 02 lists for it, then crosscut every strip to its length in the cut list. Sheet 03 sets out the rip and the truing, and draws the saw from the front for each strip. Bars are ripped a little over and then machined to size, so the saw only has to be close.
    • Test each setup on scrap first: rip three short bars, true them, and dry-fit them against a square. Half a degree of bevel error shows as a gap you can slip paper into; fix the tilt before cutting good wood.
    • The cut list has 18 bars plus 6 spares (one in ten, and at least one of every bar in every wood). Rip every bar of a shape in one run, spares included, then check a handful with calipers: all within 1/64" off the saw, and within a few thousandths of each other after the last planer pass.
    See sheet 03
    DON'Trip a strip narrower than the gap between blade and fence without a push block, or change the fence between strips of one width. Every strip of a width comes off one setting.
  4. 4
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the strip panels

    • Log A. Glue 2 x bar 1 (walnut), 4 x bar 2 (maple), 3 x bar 2 (walnut) as drawn on sheet 04. Make 1 at 19-1/4" long, for 7 slices plus 1 spare.
    • Log B. Glue 2 x bar 1 (maple), 4 x bar 2 (walnut), 3 x bar 2 (maple) as drawn on sheet 04. Make 1 at 17-1/4" long, for 6 slices plus 1 spare.
    • Square-edged strips are the one case where many parts can be glued at once: lay the strips in order on taped cauls, glue the edges, clamp across the panel every 6 inches with a pair of cauls, one above and one below, clamped near each end to keep it flat. Squiggled faces up and down, planed edges to the glue.
    • Glue one panel at a time unless you have clamps and cauls for two.
    • Scrape when rubbery; leave overnight.
    See sheet 04
  5. 5

    True the strip panels

    • Plane both faces of each panel clean and parallel, all panels at one planer setting. These faces are the glue faces of the board. The strips were milled 1/8" over for this: take about half of it off each face and bring the panels to 1-1/4" thick. Set the planer with calipers on the panel.
    • The panels are 11-1/2" wide, which a 12-1/2" planer takes.
    DON'Tleave a glue face as it came out of the clamps. Dried glue and a part standing a few thousandths proud hold the next joint open.
  6. 6

    Crosscut the slices

    • Square one end of each panel first. Lay the panel against the sled fence and clamp it down.
    • Set the stop block 2-7/8" from the blade and use the 1" spacer: slide the panel up to the spacer, clamp it, take the spacer away, then cut. The slice comes off 1-7/8" thick (finished 1-3/4" plus 1/8" to flatten) with an inch of room beside it, so it cannot bind between blade and stop.
    • Cut 15 slices in all: 7 + 1 spare from panel A, 6 + 1 spare from panel B. Pencil the panel letter on the end grain of each slice and keep two stacks.
    • Hold the slice down on the sled base with a push stick as the blade comes through, hand well clear. Push the sled on until the slice is past the blade, stop, and lift the slice away before pulling the sled back.
    • The last 2" of each panel is the part the clamp holds. It is never sliced: stop there. Lengths carry 1/2" of slack for a second squaring cut.
    DON'Tcut a slice against a fixed stop with no spacer, or slice into the last 2 inches of a log. A trapped slice is thrown; a short stub cannot be held.
  7. 7

    Lay out the slices

    • The slices are the rows. 13 of them. Lay them out end grain up in the order on sheet 05: slices of panel A and panel B take turns, and the first and last rows are both panel A. No slice is turned over or end for end. The faces that touch are the planed faces of the panels.
    • Look at the whole board before any glue goes on: squares line up down the length, and the corners match.
    • Take the saw fuzz off the sawn arrises of each slice with one stroke of 220 paper on a block.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tspread glue before the whole board lies dry in order and the pattern looks right.
  8. 8
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the rows into the board

    • Dry-fit the 13 rows in order and line up the squares along a straightedge clamped to the bench at one side, then draw a pencil line across every joint.
    • Glue three rows at a time (13 rows make 4 sections, the last of 4), then glue the sections together. Put a taped scrap strip between the clamp jaws and the outside rows, so the jaws never mark the board. Clamp across the rows every 4 to 5 inches with moderate pressure, with a pair of taped cauls, one above and one below, clamped across the rows near each end to keep them in one plane.
    • The faces being glued are long grain (the planed faces of the panels): one thin rolled coat on one face of each joint. The pencil lines hold the register: check them after the clamps are on and tap a row back if it has crept.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery. Leave the board in clamps overnight and out of clamps a day before flattening.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue every row at once. More than three joints open at a time creep out of plane and out of register before the clamps are on.
  9. 9

    Flatten, then trim

    • Flatten first, trim second. Flatten both faces with a drum sander (60 or 80 grit, light passes) or a router sled (wedge the board in a frame, spoilboard bit, passes of 1/32" or less, outside edges first), then a random-orbit sander; a router sled does the whole job if there is no drum sander. The slices were cut 1/8" thick of finished for this. Stop at 1-3/4".
    • Then trim to 11-1/4" x 16-1/4" with a track saw or on the crosscut sled. Only the two edges where the slices end are sawn: 1/8" off the left and 1/8" off the right, parallel, which brings the two outside strips, made 1/8" over width for this, to the same width as the rest. Mark each cut from the nearest glue line, not from the uneven edge: one finished square outside that line. Each cut is 16-1/4" long and takes about one kerf: a track saw on its rail does it best; a crosscut sled only if it takes 16-1/4" in front of the blade. The other two edges are planed faces of the first and last slice. The clamps bore on scrap strips, not on them, so they are not sawn: scrape off any glue and sand them with the faces, and the end squares stay full size. If an end is marked anyway, take a 1/32" skim off both ends alike. Tape the cut line and back the exit edge with scrap so the end grain does not chip.
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let it dry, then sand 220.
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain breaks out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  10. 10

    Edges and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1/8" round-over and break the corners: sharp end-grain edges chip.
    • Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners, so the board dries evenly underneath.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
