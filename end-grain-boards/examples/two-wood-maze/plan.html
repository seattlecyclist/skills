Overview

A maze of pale maple bars in dark walnut: short bars that each step sideways once on a 45 degree jog, in square tiles turned a quarter turn in a checkerboard so the bars seem to pass over and under one another
15-15/16" × 12" × 1-1/2"
16 of 16 checks pass

How it goes together

  • The whole board is one square tile, 2" on a side: two maple bars 1/2" wide in a walnut ground, each stepping up one bar width through a 45 degree jog. Every other tile is turned a quarter turn. The maze, and the look of bars passing over and under, come from that alone.
  • The tile is the end of a log made of four identical slabs. A slab is 1/2" thick with the maple and walnut meeting on a 45 degree line, and it is ripped off a two-ply blank with the blade tilted to 45 degrees.
  • What repeats: 1 slab shape, 1 log type and 1 plank type make all 6 rows. Every row is a crosscut of the one plank, as it comes off the saw or turned end for end.
  • Why: rows repeat every 2 rows as cut; a row turned end for end is the row next to it (the same as sliding it one tile along); so 1 plank type makes all 6 rows, 1 log type makes the plank and 1 slab type makes every log.
  • Nothing is assembled from small pieces. Blanks, slabs, logs and the plank are all long, flat-sided and planed between glue-ups; the rows are crosscut from the finished plank.
  • The base board has no border: the pattern runs to the edge. The dark border on the maker’s board is an option on sheet 07.

Tools

  • Calipers and a straightedge
  • Flat cauls, a straight fence caul, bar clamps, Titebond III

  • Mineral oil and board butter

Board feet include 25% for defects and milling. Each blank gives 6 slabs; the first cut wastes a triangle and the last 1-1/2" is left to keep the blank flat on the saw, so about half of each blank ends up in the board. 7/8" from 4/4 stock needs boards that arrive a full inch thick and flat; otherwise buy 5/4. Narrower boards can be edge-glued to 9-1/4" first.

The woods

  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood; a little soft, so keep it to accents or pair it with cherry.

The maker says only that the board is two woods. From the photos the dark one is walnut and the light one looks like ash (strong ring lines); that is my reading, not the maker’s statement. Ash end grain has open pores, so this plan uses hard maple by default. With ash the bars show more of the light and shade seen in the video.

The pattern

Worked out from the maker’s photos, then checked against them
tile 2" · bar 1/2"

What the photos show

  • Bars run only along the two edges of the board and step sideways on 45 degree jogs. (At phone resolution the jogs read as 60 degrees; in the full-size stills they measure 45.)
  • Glue lines every four bar widths, both ways, mark square tiles. Every tile holds the same two stepped bars; every other tile is turned a quarter turn.
  • The maker’s field is 8 tiles by 4 inside a dark border. This plan keeps the 8 tiles across and uses 6 rows to make a 16" × 12" board with no border.
  • Check: the model on this sheet, drawn 8 × 4 and laid over a squared-up still of the finished board, agrees with it on 90% of pixels; the rest is along bar edges, from lens distortion and wood figure.
  • The depth in the video comes from end-grain figure: turning a tile a quarter turn turns its growth rings too, so neighbouring bars catch the light differently.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
12 steps · 4 glue-ups
  1. 1

    Mill the plies

    • Joint and plane 3 maple boards to 1" thick and 3 walnut boards to 7/8" thick, all 9-1/4" wide and 31" long. Plane each wood in one session at one setting.
    • The two thicknesses set where the light and dark meet in every bar of the pattern, so measure them with calipers: within 0.005" of each other board to board.
    • No 9-1/4" boards? Edge-glue narrower ones to that width first. A seam inside a ply never shows as a line in the pattern, only as an ordinary glue line in one wood.
  2. 2
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the two-ply blanks

    • Glue one maple board face to face on one walnut board: 3 blanks, each 1-7/8" thick. Roll the glue on one face, rub the boards together, and clamp between flat cauls with a clamp every 4 to 5 inches.
    • Next day scrape the squeeze-out, joint one edge of each blank square to its faces and rip the other edge parallel. That jointed edge rides the fence in the next step.
  3. 3

    Rip the slabs with the blade at 45 degrees

    • Tilt the blade to 45 degrees with a digital gauge, away from the fence, and raise it to cut through the 1-7/8" blank (the blade is in 2.65" of wood). Riving knife on, featherboard ahead of the blade, push blocks.
    • The blank lies flat on the table with its jointed edge against the fence for every cut. The slab is the piece that comes off on the far side of the blade.
    • First cut. Set the fence so the blade just takes the top corner off the far edge. That waste is a triangle.
    • Then move the fence 31/32" (0.972) toward the blade for each slab. Each slab comes off about 9/16" thick with two bevelled edges. Check the first with calipers and adjust the move.
    • Take 6 slabs from each blank and stop: the last 1-1/2" of the blank is what holds it flat. 18 slabs in all; 16 are needed, the rest are for setting up step 5.
    DON'Trip the last slab from a blank that no longer sits flat with its edge on the fence.
  4. 4

    Plane the slabs

    • A slab lies flat on its wide face, so it goes through the planer like any thin board. Plane every slab to exactly 1/2", taking the same from each face, all at the last setting in one session.
    • Check. Four slabs stacked measure 2". The stack height is the size of the tile, and the next step rips the slabs to that same width.
  5. 5

    Rip the slabs square, twice

    • Blade back to 90 degrees. Each slab needs both bevelled edges ripped off square, at the right distance from its glue line.
    • Cut A squares the maple edge. Lay the slab so the sharp corner of its walnut edge is down on the table and against the fence, and set the fence 2-19/32" (2.591) from the blade. Test on a spare slab and measure the maple from the new edge to the glue line: 55/64" (0.854) on the face that was up, 1-23/64" (1.354) on the face that was down. Nudge the fence until both are right, then run every slab.
    • Cut B squares the walnut edge. Turn the slab so the new square edge is against the fence and rip it to 2" wide, or to the height of four stacked slabs if that measured a hair different. The log must come out square.
    • After both cuts every slab is 2" x 1/2" and shows 1-23/64" (1.354) of maple and 41/64" (0.646) of walnut on one face, 55/64" (0.854) and 1-9/64" (1.146) on the other.
  6. 6
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the logs: four slabs each

    • Stack four slabs, rolling every other one over along its length, so the maple is on the left in slabs 1 and 3 and on the right in slabs 2 and 4. Looked at from the end, all four glue lines lean the same way and the maple makes two bars that each step up once.
    • Dry-stack first and look at the end against the drawing. If the bars do not join into two clean steps, a slab is end for end instead of rolled.
    • Glue on a flat base with the edges pushed against a straight, taped fence caul so the four stay flush. Clamp down through a caul every 4 inches, then lightly across. 4 logs, 30" long.
  7. 7

    True the logs and cut them in two

    • Scrape the glue, joint one side of each log, and plane the opposite side until the log is as wide as it is tall, within 0.005". Take a skim only: every pass narrows the bars at that face.
    • Crosscut each log into two lengths of 14-3/4": 8 logs.
  8. 8
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue the plank in two halves

    • Lay the logs side by side and give every other one a quarter turn, so the ends read flat, upright, flat, upright. All the flat ones match each other and all the upright ones match each other; check the end view against the drawing before any glue.
    • Glue 4 logs at a time on flat cauls, so each part is 8" wide and fits a 12.5" planer. Plane both faces of each part with the lightest pass that cleans them, to the same thickness.
    • These faces are the glue faces between rows later, so they need to be flat and clean, but each pass also thins the outer bars. Stop as soon as the face is clean.
    • Then glue the parts together edge to edge on the same cauls, faces flush, into one plank 16" wide and 14-3/4" long. Scrape the one new glue line flush.
  9. 9

    Crosscut the plank into rows

    • On the crosscut sled with a stop block, square one end of the plank, then cut 7 slices 1-5/8" thick. Each slice is one whole row of the board. 6 are needed and one is a spare.
    • Hold the plank down and against the sled fence for every cut and stop when the piece left is too short to hold safely. Number the slices in order and pencil an arrow on the same edge of each.
  10. 10
    GLUE-UP 4

    Lay out and glue the board

    • Stand the slices on edge, end grain up, all with the same cut face up. Turn every other slice end for end, flat on the bench. Never flip one over: its other face is the mirror image.
    • Each tile now sits beside tiles turned the other way, and the bars meet in pinwheels at the tile corners as in the drawing. To match the maker’s board, the flat tiles' bars step up to the right. If yours step down, flip every slice over.
    • Glue the rows with their ends flush against a stop, on taped cauls, clamps across the rows every 4 inches and cauls above to keep them level. These are long-grain faces: one coat of glue, normal pressure.
  11. 11

    Flatten

    • Router sled first, then the random-orbit sander: 80 grit to remove the router marks, then 120, 180, 220. Raise the grain with a damp cloth before the last grit.
    • A drum sander does the same job if you have one. For the planer route, read sheet 07 before trying it.
    See sheet 07
  12. 12

    Trim, ease the edges, finish

    • Take 1/32" off each end of the rows on the crosscut sled with a backer, just enough to clean them. The other two edges are planed faces and need only sanding. Finished size 15-15/16" x 12" x 1-1/2".
    • 1/8 in. round-over on every edge. Flood with mineral oil until it stops soaking in, then board butter. Add four rubber feet.
07

Flattening & border

Two ways to flatten without a drum sander, and the maker's dark border as an add-on
options

Route 1 · router sled and sander

  • Board on a flat base, shimmed so it cannot rock, between two straight rails; a wide straight or bowl bit in a router on a bridge.
  • Passes of 1/32 in. or less, the full face, then the other face.
  • Sand out the router marks from 80 grit. This is the route the plan assumes and the one every source agrees on.

Route 2 · helical-head planer

  • Up to here the planer has done everything, because every part was long grain. The finished board is end grain, and a planer can tear the trailing edge out or throw the board.
  • If you use it anyway, follow George Vondriska's method (sheet 08): glue sacrificial long-grain rails to the leading and trailing edges or put a heavy chamfer on the trailing edge, take 1/64 in. or less a pass, sharp inserts, and stand out of line.
  • This plan has not tested it and does not need it. With the optional border on, the border is the sacrificial edge: plane before the final trim.

Optional · the dark border

  • The maker’s board has a plain walnut border about two and a half bar widths wide: 1-1/4" here, for a finished board of 18-7/16" × 14-1/2".
  • Make it end grain like the field: a walnut board planed to 1-1/4", crosscut into blocks 1-5/8" long, stood on end and glued along the long edges first, then the ends.
  • Never a long-grain frame: the field moves across its width with the seasons and a long-grain frame does not, so one of them cracks.
  • To add it to this plan, put "frame": {"width": 1.25, "species": "walnut"} in design.json and rebuild.

Worked out for this plan, not shown

  • I could not play either video here, only read their titles and the still frames YouTube publishes for them. The order of the maker’s steps between those stills is not known to me.
  • The two-ply blank and its thicknesses (1" and 7/8"), the 45 degree slab, and the four-slab log are my reconstruction. They reproduce the face exactly on paper and use the operations the maker’s stills show, but the maker’s own laminate may have more layers or a different order.
  • Squaring each slab with its sharp corner against the fence (step 5) is my method. Prove the fence setting on the spare slabs before cutting the rest.
  • Gluing whole logs into one plank and crosscutting rows from it is the standard plank method, applied here; in the last still the maker appears to glue loose blocks instead.
  • The helical-planer route for the finished board is Vondriska's, untested on this board.
  • Nobody has built this plan yet.
