Overview

Eight-point quilt stars: each star has eight diamond points alternating padauk and purpleheart on a hard maple ground, the stars set in a grid

About the pattern

  • 5 x 4 LeMoyne star blocks, 3.414" square. Each block is four identical quarter units turned 90 degrees to each other, so one block log makes every block.
  • The edge triangles are made as two half-triangles (one in each quarter); the glue line between them is same-species and disappears in end grain.

What you'll need

  • African padauk: 2.5 bd ft
  • Purpleheart: 2.5 bd ft
  • Hard maple: 6.5 bd ft

Tools

  • Table saw with a digital angle gauge (blade at 45°)
  • Thin-rip jig, zero-clearance insert, push block
  • Crosscut sled with a stop block
  • Bar clamps, cauls, packing tape for the bevelled units
  • Router sled or drum sander (not a planer)
  • Random-orbit sander, mineral oil and board butter

Board feet include 25% for defects and milling. Blanks are 51⁄2" wide after jointing; wider stock gives more bars per blank.

The woods

  • African padauk · Janka 1970 · shrinkage 5.2%. Orange-red; dust is an irritant, and it can bleed red into maple: wipe with solvent before glue.
  • Purpleheart · Janka 2520 · shrinkage 6.4%. Vivid purple that browns with UV; very hard on tools.
  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
8 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (African padauk, Purpleheart, Hard maple) and let them sit in the shop a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained, quartersawn or rift stock for the bars where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
  2. 2

    Rip the bars

    • Joint and plane every blank flat and to its exact thickness (material list). Bars of one shape must come from blanks planed in the same session at the same setting, or they won't match.
    • Bar A (45-degree rhombus, 1" side): Mill the blank to 0.707" (≈23⁄32") thick (the height of the 45-degree parallelogram). Tilt the blade 45.0 degrees from vertical (set it with a digital angle gauge, not the saw's scale). Rip the first bevel to establish the edge and discard the strip. Without changing the tilt, rip each bar off the same edge: 1.000" (≈1") from the blade at the table (a thin-rip jig against the blank's bevelled edge makes every bar identical). Each pass cuts the next bar's leading edge, so bars come off as parallelograms with no flipping. Check the first bar: 45.0 and 135.0 degree corners, every side 1.000" (≈1") on the slant.
    • Bar B (1" square): Mill the blank to 1.000" (≈1") thick. Rip bars 1.000" (≈1") wide, blade square to the table.
    • Bar C (right isosceles triangle, 1" base): Mill the blank to 0.500" (≈1⁄2") thick (the triangle's height). Tilt the blade 45.0 degrees and rip the first bevel; discard the strip. Flip the blank over (face for face) before every pass so the bevels alternate; each pass releases a triangle 1.000" (≈1") across its base with the base on the table side of the cut. Check: equal sides 0.707" (≈23⁄32"), apex 90.0 degrees.
    • Rip every bar of a shape in one session without touching the saw setup, and rip 10% spares. Dry-fit the first few bars into a unit or a short row before ripping the rest: half a degree of bevel error doubles at every joint and opens gaps across a row.
    See sheet 03
    DON'Ttouch the blade tilt or the thin-rip jig partway through a shape. Bars from two setups never meet cleanly; rip every bar of a shape, plus spares, in one run.
  3. 3
    1ST GLUE-UP

    Glue the units

    • Unit U1: glue 5 bars as drawn; make 8 x 203⁄4" long (for Log L).
    • Where a unit is needed at several lengths, glue it long and crosscut the pieces from it.
    • Bevelled bars won't take bar clamps. Lay the bars face down edge to edge on packing tape in the drawn order, glue the bevels, fold them up so the tape pulls the joints closed, and wrap with more tape or rubber bands. Check each unit with the template from the diagram while the glue is wet.
    • Let the glue (Titebond III, or II) cure overnight, then scrape the squeeze-out flush; the units' outside faces are glue faces for the next step.
    See sheet 04
  4. 4
    2ND GLUE-UP

    Glue the logs

    • Log L: glue 4 units side by side as drawn; make 2 logs, 20.750" (≈203⁄4") long, for 20 slices plus 2 spare.
    • Glue on a flat, waxed or papered surface with cauls across the faces so the bars stay in plane; a log that is twisted gives slices that won't sit flat together.
    • Scrape the glue off, then square one end of each log.
    See sheet 04
  5. 5

    Crosscut the slices

    • Before cutting, draw a pencil V across the top of each log: every slice keeps a mark that says which way is up and where it came from.
    • On a crosscut sled with a stop block, cut slices 1.625" (≈15⁄8") thick (finished 11⁄2" plus 1⁄8" to flatten). Hold the off-cut side with a hold-down, never the fence side.
    • Cut 22 slices in all; stack them in order.
  6. 6
    3RD GLUE-UP

    Glue the board

    • Lay out the 20 slices as numbered in the layout drawing. Every slice goes down as cut. Dry-fit the whole board and look at it before any glue.
    • Glue in stages of two to four slices, then glue the stages together. End grain drinks glue: brush a thin coat on both faces, wait two minutes, add a second coat, then clamp with moderate pressure and cauls top and bottom so the slices stay flush (the faces are end grain and are hard to plane flat).
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue all the slices at once. With more than four joints the slices creep out of plane under clamp pressure, and end grain is slow to flatten.
  7. 7

    Trim and flatten

    • Trim to 13.657" (≈1321⁄32") x 17.071" (≈171⁄16") on the red lines (crosscut sled or track saw, with a backer to stop breakout).
    • Flatten both faces with a router sled or a drum sander. Do not run end grain through a thickness planer: it blows out the trailing edge and can throw pieces.
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let dry, sand 220.
    See sheet 01
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain blows out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  8. 8

    Edges, feet and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1⁄8" chamfer and break the corners: end-grain edges chip if left sharp.
    • Cut finger grips in both ends with a 11⁄4" cove or core-box bit on the router table.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (end grain takes a lot: several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
