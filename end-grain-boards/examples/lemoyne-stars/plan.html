Overview

Eight-point quilt stars: each star has eight diamond points alternating red padauk and dark walnut on a hard maple ground, the stars set in a grid with a maple margin, a thin walnut line and a maple end-grain frame
13-7/32" × 18-7/32" × 1-1/2"
17 of 18 checks pass

How it goes together

  • Each star block is built as a square log: two bars make a band, two bands and a square bar make a quarter log, two quarters make a half and two halves the block. The faces of each glue-up that are glued next are machined flat first, then the block logs are sliced and the slices glued in rows.
  • 6 x 4 star blocks, each 2.561" square. A block is four identical quarter logs turned 90 degrees to each other.
  • The ground triangles on each block edge are made as two halves, one in each quarter; that glue line joins the same wood and disappears in end grain.

The woods

  • African padauk · Janka 1970 · shrinkage 5.2%. Orange-red; dust is an irritant, and it can bleed red into maple: wipe with solvent before glue.
  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood; a little soft, so keep it to accents or pair it with cherry.
  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
03

Bars & rip setups

Every piece of the face is the end of one of these bars. Each drawing shows the section, then the table saw from the front for that rip
62 bars + spares · kerf 1/8"

The bevel rip

The sled. Every bevelled bar is cut on the rip sled (sheet 06, step 2), the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps that the builders in the best practices guide use. It runs against the rip fence, which stays on the side the blade leans away from: its usual place on a left-tilt saw; on a right-tilt saw move the fence to the left of the blade.

Two decks. The fence deck, between the fence and the blade, carries the locating fence and the toggle clamps. The landing deck, beyond the blade and under its lean, catches what comes off. Nothing is ever loose on the saw table.

Trapezoids and diamonds. They start as square-edged strips and are clamped to the fence deck for both bevels; only a waste sliver drops on the landing deck.

Triangles. A triangle leaves no flat for a clamp, so the wide blank is clamped and the bar is cut free on the landing deck. It is not loose: the stop strip's edge is ripped at the same tilt and leans over the bar's sloping face, so the bar sits base down in a dovetail-shaped pocket between stop and deck, on stick-on sandpaper, and cannot lift, tip or creep. It rides along with the sled; nothing rubs it past the blade.

After every pass. Push the sled on until the work is clear of the blade, or switch off and wait for the blade to stop, before lifting anything out. Keep the riving knife in place.

The cards below. Each gives what differs for that bar: the strip to start from, how it is turned for the second bevel, and the figure to set the sled to.

3

45-degree diamond, 3/4" faces

blade 45.0° from vertical
  1. Mill the blank to 0.530" (≈17/32") thick (the bar's height lying on its 3/4" face).
  2. Blade square. Rip square-edged strips 1.431" (≈1-7/16") wide, the ordinary way, fence 1-7/16" from the blade.
  3. Tilt the blade 45.0 degrees from vertical; set it with a digital angle gauge, not the saw's scale. Cut on the rip sled as in The bevel rip at the top of this sheet; this bar's setup is drawn above. Clamp a strip against the locating fence and bevel one edge. Then roll the strip over (the same end leading, the other face up) so the sawn edge is against the locating fence, its sharp corner down on the deck, and bevel the other edge. The two bevels come out parallel. Set the locating fence with test strips until the face lying on the deck measures 0.838" (≈27/32") (3/4" plus the allowance); that one setting does both passes.
  4. Leave the two sawn faces as they are. They carry the allowance and are planed with the band after it is glued; the two planed faces are the glue faces.
  5. Check: corners 45 and 135 degrees, the two planed faces measure 0.838" (≈27/32") as sawn and come to 3/4" once the sawn faces are machined.
4

right isosceles triangle, 3/4" base

blade 45.0° from vertical
  1. Mill the blank to 0.419" (≈13/32") thick (the triangle's height plus the allowance).
  2. Tilt the blade 45.0 degrees from vertical (digital angle gauge). Cut on the rip sled as in The bevel rip at the top of this sheet; this bar's setup is drawn above. Clamp the blank on the fence deck with its free edge over the slot and rip one bevel along it; that strip is waste.
  3. For every bar: turn the blank over end for end (the same edge stays toward the blade, the other face goes down), slide it across until its sharp lower edge touches the landing stop, close the clamps on it and push the sled through. The bar is left on the landing deck, base down and point up, between the stop and the blade. Screw the landing stop with its sharp lower edge 0.838" (≈27/32") from the blade at the deck, then confirm on a test bar. Plane the stop strip to 1/4" thick, lower than this bar, so the blade passes over it. The blank's bevelled edge slides in under the stop's leaning face until the two faces meet. Back the blank for every pass: fill the gap between its rear edge and the locating fence with spacer strips ripped from scrap to suit (the gap grows by about one bar's width every pass, so keep the offcut strips and add one each time), so the clamps are not the only thing holding it in place. Stop when less than 1-1/2" of blank is left; that strip is waste.
  4. Leave the two sawn faces as they are. They end up on the outside of the glue-up and are machined with it; the planed base is the glue face.
  5. Check: 90 degrees at the point; the base is 0.838" (≈27/32") as sawn and 3/4" once the glue-up is trued.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
18 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (African padauk, Black walnut, Hard maple) and let them sit in the shop for a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained stock, quartersawn or rift where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
    See sheet 02
  2. 2

    Make the jigs

    • Rip sled. For every bevelled bar. It is the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps from the best practices guide. Start with a base of 1/2" plywood about 34" x 16", one long edge straight to run against the rip fence. Glue and screw a bridge of 3/4" x 4" hardwood, standing on edge, across each end. Tilt the blade to 45 degrees, raise it fully, set the rip fence 9" from it and push the sled through: the blade saws the base into two decks and notches both bridges, which are tall enough to stay whole above the cut and hold the decks together.
    • Fence deck. The 9" deck beside the rip fence. Screw a straight locating fence, 3/4" x 1-1/2", flat on it and parallel to the cut, through slotted holes so it can be reset for each bar shape, and mount three toggle clamps behind it with their pads reaching over it. The pads close on a caul, a straight strip 1/2" wide and 3/4" high laid along the top of the work, so the pressure is spread and no pad is near the blade. Lay the caul over the part of the work that has deck under it, toward the locating fence, never out over a leaning edge.
    • Landing deck. The other deck. For triangle bars a stop strip is screwed to it parallel to the cut: a 3/4" strip with one edge ripped at the blade's tilt, set so that edge leans toward the blade and over the bar, with a strip of stick-on 120 grit sandpaper on the deck in front of it. Leave the rip fence where it is for the whole run; every setting is made on the sled.
    • Band cradle. A flat base of 3/4" MDF with packing tape on it, and a stop strip screwed along one side whose inner edge is ripped at 45 degrees so it leans over the work. The diamond bar's free bevel tucks under that edge. A straight loose caul on the other side takes the clamps, and a taped caul on top keeps the band flat (drawing on sheet 04).
    • Corner cauls. Two L-shaped cauls, each two strips of 3/4" plywood glued at exactly 90 degrees with inside legs 1" wide, inside faces taped, as long as the logs. The legs are narrower than the log so the two cauls never touch each other before the log is tight.
    • Row jig. A flat melamine or waxed MDF base about 19" x 9" with one straight fence and an end stop square to it. A loose second fence is pushed up to the slices and held by wedges against a cleat.
    • Row truing carrier. A flat board with two straight, square rails screwed down 1-5/8" apart (a slice thick), about 2-1/4" high. A glued row stands on edge between the rails with its top edge proud. Make a second, low carrier the same way with rails 3/8" high for the two border rows.
    • Crosscut sled. With a hold-down clamp, a stop block and a 1" spacer. Check it cuts square in both directions.
  3. 3

    Mill the blanks and rip the bars

    • Joint one face and one edge of every blank, then plane to thickness: 0.419" (≈13/32"), 0.530" (≈17/32"), 3/4" (material list, sheet 02). Set the planer with calipers, not a tape, and plane every blank of one thickness in the same session without moving the planer between them.
    • Crosscut the blanks to the lengths in the cut list. Then rip and true each bar shape as set out on sheet 03, which draws the saw and the rip sled from the front for every bevelled bar. Bars are ripped a little over and then machined to size, so the saw only has to be close.
    • Test each setup on scrap first: rip three short bars, true them, and dry-fit them against a square. Half a degree of bevel error shows as a gap you can slip paper into; fix the tilt before cutting good wood.
    • The cut list has 62 bars plus 10 spares (one in ten, and at least one of every bar in every wood). Rip every bar of a shape in one run, spares included, then check a handful with calipers: all within 1/64".
    See sheet 03
    DON'Trip a bevel with the work loose on the saw table, or with a bar between the tilted blade and the fence. Every bevel is cut with the work clamped to the rip sled. Stop when a triangle blank is narrower than 1-1/2"; that last strip is waste.
  4. 4
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the bands

    • Log A. Glue bar 3 (padauk), bar 4 (maple) as drawn on sheet 04. Make 12 at 20" long.
    • Log B. Glue bar 3 (walnut), bar 4 (maple) as drawn on sheet 04. Make 12 at 20" long.
    • Dry-fit in the band cradle. The diamond bar lies on a sawn face with its free bevel tucked under the 45 degree stop; the triangle bar sits beside it on a sawn face, its planed base against the diamond's other bevel. The two planed faces are the joint.
    • Spread a thin, even coat of Titebond III with a small roller on one face of each joint. Put painter's tape on the outside faces beside the joints so squeeze-out lifts off. Push the triangle in with the loose caul and clamps across the cradle every 4 to 5 inches: the push wedges the triangle under the diamond's bevel and closes the joint. Clamp a taped caul down on top so neither bar can ride up.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery and leave overnight. Pencil a mark on one end of every band; every later log carries that marked end, and it always faces you. When the glue has cured, square both ends on the crosscut sled, taking only what cleans them.
    See sheet 04
  5. 5

    True the bands

    • Plane both broad faces of each band, equal cuts from each face, all bands at one planer setting, down to 0.530" (≈17/32") thick. The sawn faces of the bars carried the allowance for this. A band has one bevelled edge that ends in a feather. Nest the bands in pairs: lay one wide face down and roll its partner over, wide face up, the same end leading, so the two bevels lap and the pair is a rectangle. Lay the pair on a flat carrier board between two straight rails screwed down a snug fit apart and lower than the bands, and tape across both ends: the rails stop the lapped bevels from wedging apart under the feed rollers, the pair goes through as a rectangle, and no thin edge leads into the knives. Turn the pair over as one to plane the other faces.
    • Then bring every band to width at one setting, working from its solid edge. First take one light jointer pass on the square edge (the triangle bar's outer face) to clean it. Then put the band on the rip sled with the blade still at 45 degrees: wide face up under the caul, the jointed square edge against the locating fence, and skim the bevelled edge so the wide face measures 1.280" (≈1-9/32"). Set the locating fence once with a test band and run all the bands at that setting. The skim also leaves the bevel, which is the next glue face, freshly cut. This width decides whether the corner cauls close the diagonal joints of the star, so check a few bands with calipers: all within a few thousandths.
    DON'Tleave a glue face as it came out of the clamps. Dried glue and a part standing a few thousandths proud hold the next joint open.
  6. 6
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the quarter logs

    • Log C. Glue log A, log B, bar 2 (maple) as drawn on sheet 04. Make 12 at 19-1/2" long.
    • Dry-fit in the corner cauls. The two bands lie in the lower caul, one against each leg, with their star bars meeting on the diagonal at the caul's corner; the square bar fills the open corner on top.
    • Look at the end against sheet 04: the two star woods are side by side at the corner. A band with a prime (B′) is turned end for end.
    • Spread a thin, even coat of Titebond III with a small roller on one face of each joint. Put painter's tape on the outside faces beside the joints so squeeze-out lifts off. Seat the parts in the lower caul, close the second caul over the square bar, and clamp both ways every 4 to 5 inches. Sight both ends: the diagonal joint must run exactly corner to corner.
    • Scrape when rubbery; leave overnight. When the glue has cured, square both ends on the crosscut sled, taking only what cleans them.
    See sheet 04
  7. 7

    True the quarter logs

    • True the two inner faces, the ones that lay in the lower caul and meet at the star corner: one light jointer pass each, about 0.01", keeping them square to each other. Leave the two outer faces alone until the block is whole. The quarter logs come out about 1.270" (≈1-9/32") square.
  8. 8
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue the half logs

    • Log D. Glue 2 x log C as drawn on sheet 04. Make 6 at 19" long.
    • Glue two quarter logs side by side so their star corners touch and the two faces in the joint are of different star woods (sheet 04). Lay both with a trued star-corner face down on a flat taped surface, star corners touching at the joint; those trued faces are the register, not the outer faces. Clamp across the joint between flat taped cauls every 4 to 5 inches.
    • Line up the star points at the joint at both ends before the clamps go tight.
    • Scrape when rubbery; leave overnight. When the glue has cured, square both ends on the crosscut sled, taking only what cleans them.
    See sheet 04
  9. 9

    True the half logs

    • True the one long face that carries both star corners (the next glue face): a light jointer pass, about 0.01". Check it is flat and square to the ends. Halves are then about 1.260" (≈1-1/4") x 2.541" (≈2-17/32").
  10. 10
    GLUE-UP 4

    Glue the block logs

    • Log E. Glue 2 x log D as drawn on sheet 04. Make 3 at 18-1/2" long, for 24 slices plus 3 spare.
    • Log F is one solid bar (bar 1): nothing to glue. Make 2 at 23-3/4" long, for 22 slices plus 2 spare, and true it with the other logs.
    • Glue two half logs with their star centres together. Keep the marked ends of both halves toward you and roll the second half half a turn about its length, so the star woods alternate all the way round; do not turn it end for end. Eight star points must meet at the centre: line them up at both ends, clamp lightly, look again, then tighten.
    • Scrape when rubbery; leave overnight. When the glue has cured, square both ends on the crosscut sled, taking only what cleans them.
    See sheet 04
    DON'Tglue all four quarter logs at once. Two joints at right angles under clamps slide; halves first, then the block.
  11. 11

    True the block logs

    • Joint one face of each block log, joint an adjacent face square to it, then plane the other two faces parallel, every log through the planer at one setting. Take as little as cleans each face, about 0.01". The block logs finish about 2.501" (≈2-1/2") across the last glue line and 2.521" (≈2-17/32") along it; that they all match within a few thousandths matters more than the figure. Chalk one of the two faces that show the last glue line running down their middle (they are the faces 2.501" (≈2-1/2") wide). At the saw that chalked face goes down on the sled and it becomes a long edge of the row, so every slice is 2.501" (≈2-1/2") along the row.
    • Check each log is square at both ends.
    • Plane the border bar (Log F) to the same width as the block logs at the same planer setting (2.521" (≈2-17/32")), so its slices line up with theirs.
  12. 12

    Crosscut the slices

    • Square the unmarked end of each log first. Lay the log on its chalked face against the sled fence, marked end away from the stop, and clamp it down.
    • Set the stop block 2-5/8" from the blade and use the 1" spacer: slide the log up to the spacer, clamp the log, take the spacer away, then cut. The slice comes off 1-5/8" thick (finished 1-1/2" plus 1/8" to flatten) with an inch of room beside it, so it cannot bind between blade and stop.
    • Cut 51 slices in all: 24 + 3 spare from log E, 22 + 2 spare from log F. As each slice comes off, turn up the face that was toward the blade and pencil a short line on it along the edge that lay on the sled. That line is the mark sheet 05 draws inside every slice. Stack the slices by letter.
    • Hold the slice down on the sled base with a push stick as the blade comes through, hand well clear. Push the sled on until the slice is past the blade, stop, and lift the slice away before pulling the sled back.
    • The last 2" of each log is the part the clamp holds. It is never sliced: stop there.
    DON'Tcut a slice against a fixed stop with no spacer, or slice into the last 2 inches of a log. A trapped slice is thrown; a short stub cannot be held.
  13. 13
    GLUE-UP 5

    Glue the slices into rows

    • Lay out the whole board dry on a flat surface from sheet 05: 8 rows of up to 6 slices. Each slice goes pencil line up, with the line on the edge where the drawing shows it. A letter with a prime (A′) is turned over, line underneath; the drawing still shows which edge the line is on. Look at the whole pattern before any glue goes on.
    • The glue faces here are the trued sides of the logs: long grain. Roll a thin coat on one face of each joint and keep glue off the end grain.
    • Glue the slices of each row against the fixed fence of the row jig, two at a time, then the pairs to each other, clamping along the row with the loose fence wedged and a caul on top. Line up the star points across each joint before tightening.
    • The two border rows are slices of the border bar laid end to end; glue them the same way.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Ttrust a row that rocks in the jig. A slice that sits proud of the fence line will hold the next row off; take it out and find out why.
  14. 14

    True the rows

    • Scrape the glue from each row. Its two long edges are the glue faces for the board, and every one of them gets machined.
    • Stand the row on edge in the truing carrier, scribble pencil along the top edge, and pass it under the drum sander until the scribble just goes, about 0.01". Turn the row over and do the other edge. Every row gets the same passes at the same setting, so all star rows finish the same width, about 2.501" (≈2-1/2"). The two border rows are slices glued end to end as well, so true their edges the same way in the low carrier; they finish about 0.732" (≈23/32") wide.
    • No drum sander. A row's edge is cut across the grain, and a planer breaks out the last slice unless it is backed. With a helical-head planer, keep the row in the carrier, tape a scrap block of the same height tight behind the trailing end, and take passes of 1/64" or less. With straight knives, or if a test row chips, glue 80 grit paper to a flat board, hold the row against a square block, and take the same number of even strokes on every edge. Never plane a row loose, and keep rows off the jointer.
  15. 15
    GLUE-UP 6

    Glue the rows into the board

    • Dry-fit the 8 rows in order. Slide each row along its neighbour until the corners of the slices meet corner to corner along the whole joint, then draw a pencil line across every joint so the rows go back in register under glue.
    • Glue two or three rows at a time, then glue those sections together. Clamp across the rows every 4 to 5 inches with moderate pressure, with taped cauls clamped across the top and bottom near each end, inside the ragged ends, to keep the rows in one plane.
    • The faces being glued are long grain (the sides of the logs): one thin rolled coat on one face of each joint. The pencil lines hold the register: check them after the clamps are on and tap a row back if it has crept.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery. Leave the board in clamps overnight and out of clamps a day before flattening.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue every row at once. More than three joints open at a time creep out of plane and out of register before the clamps are on.
  16. 16

    Flatten, then trim

    • Flatten first, trim second. Flatten both faces with a drum sander (60 or 80 grit, light passes) or a router sled (wedge the board in a frame, spoilboard bit, passes of 1/32" or less, outside edges first), then a random-orbit sander; a router sled does the whole job if there is no drum sander. the slices were cut 1/8" thick of finished for this. Stop as soon as both faces are clean and the board is an even thickness; leave it over 1-1/2", because it is flattened once more with its frame on.
    • Then trim to 11.217" (≈11-7/32") x 16-7/32" with a track saw or on a crosscut sled. The trim lines are centred on the pattern (sheet 05 shows them against the slices): make the left cut 1/8" in from the left edge and the bottom cut 1/8" in from the bottom edge, square to each other, then cut the right and top edges parallel to them at the finished size. That leaves about 1/8" of waste on the right and 1/8" at the top. Tape the cut line and back the exit edge with scrap so the end grain does not chip.
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain breaks out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  17. 17
    GLUE-UP 7

    Add the frame

    • Round the field goes a 3/8" line of Black walnut and then a 5/8" frame of Hard maple. All of it stands on end like everything else, so it swells and shrinks with the field. A long-grain frame would split the board.
    • Making the strips. Plane the board to the thickness below with both edges jointed parallel. Crosscut blocks from it on the sled, each 1-5/8" long, with the same spacer stop and setting used for the slices; the board lies flat on the sled under a hold-down, and the last 2 inches are not cut. Stand the blocks on end and glue them edge to edge into four strips in the row jig: against the fixed fence, the loose blocks wedged up to them so the strip cannot buckle sideways, a taped caul with a weight on top, and one clamp along each strip with light pressure. These joints are long grain to long grain, and in one wood they disappear.
    • The line. Black walnut, planed 3/8" thick. 15 blocks, each 3/8" x 5-1/4" seen from above: two strips of 4 for the long sides (at least 16-23/32" long), two of 3 for the ends (at least 12-15/32"), and one spare.
    • The frame. Hard maple, planed 5/8" thick. 15 blocks, each 5/8" x 5-1/4" seen from above: two strips of 4 for the long sides (at least 17-15/32" long), two of 3 for the ends (at least 13-23/32"), and one spare.
    • Scrape each strip and pass the face that meets the board under the drum sander once (or take a few strokes on the 80 grit sanding board), so it is flat across the block joints.
    • Work one ring at a time, the line first. Glue the two long strips to the long edges, overhanging both ends, with clamps across the board every 4 to 5 inches and a taped caul over each joint to keep the strips level with the field. When the glue has set, saw the overhanging ends flush with the short edges on the sled, taking the strip ends and no more than a shaving of the field.
    • Glue the two end strips across the ends the same way, clamping along the length of the board, then saw their overhang flush with the long sides. The outside of the line is the glue face for the frame, so true it before going on: skim all four edges of the board with a light trim cut on the sled or track saw, a shaving only, so each is one flat, square face. Then add the frame strips round the line the same way.
    • Flatten both faces again with light passes until frame and field are in one plane, and stop at 1-1/2". The board is now 13.217" (≈13-7/32") x 18-7/32".
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let it dry, then sand 220.
    DON'Tframe the board with strips whose grain runs along the edge. The field moves across its width and length; long-grain strips do not, and the board cracks.
  18. 18

    Edges and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1/8" chamfer and break the corners: sharp end-grain edges chip.
    • Cut a finger grip in each end on the router table with a 3/4" core-box bit: 1/4" deep, 4" long, centred on the end and on the board's thickness. Stand the board on its end against a tall auxiliary fence, a backer board clamped behind it, with start and stop blocks clamped to the fence, and take three shallow passes, raising the bit each time. The lips left above and below the groove are 3/8" thick.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
