Each hexagon is two trapezoids from neighbouring rows; their glue line is same-species and disappears in end grain, but only if the rows land exactly.

Overview

About the pattern

  • 21 row strips 0.779" tall: half-hexagon trapezoids (1.8" and 0.9" across) alternating with 0.9" triangles. Each hexagon is two trapezoids from neighbouring rows; their glue line is same-species and disappears in end grain, but only if the rows land exactly.
  • Trapezoids and triangles are both ripped at a 30 degree tilt, flipping the blank every pass.

What you'll need

  • Black walnut: 7.5 bd ft
  • Hard maple: 2.5 bd ft
  • Cherry: 2.5 bd ft

Tools

  • Table saw with a digital angle gauge (blade at 30°)
  • Thin-rip jig, zero-clearance insert, push block
  • Crosscut sled with a stop block
  • Bar clamps, cauls, packing tape for the bevelled units
  • Router sled or drum sander (not a planer)
  • Random-orbit sander, mineral oil and board butter

The woods

  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood; a little soft, so keep it to accents or pair it with cherry.
  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
  • Cherry · Janka 950 · shrinkage 7.1%. Medium tone that darkens with light; soft side of acceptable.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
7 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (Black walnut, Hard maple, Cherry) and let them sit in the shop a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained, quartersawn or rift stock for the bars where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
  2. 2

    Rip the bars

    • Joint and plane every blank flat and to its exact thickness (material list). Bars of one shape must come from blanks planed in the same session at the same setting, or they won't match.
    • Bar A (60-degree trapezoid, 113⁄16" x 29⁄32"): Mill the blank to 0.779" (≈25⁄32") thick (the trapezoid's height). Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees from vertical (digital angle gauge) and rip the first bevel; discard the strip. Flip the blank over (face for face) before every pass so the bevels alternate; set the fence so each pass releases a trapezoid 1.800" (≈113⁄16") across its wide face and 0.900" (≈29⁄32") across its narrow face. Check: 60.0 degree base corners, slanted sides 0.900" (≈29⁄32").
    • Bar B (equilateral triangle, 29⁄32" base): Mill the blank to 0.779" (≈25⁄32") thick (the triangle's height). Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees and rip the first bevel; discard the strip. Flip the blank over (face for face) before every pass so the bevels alternate; each pass releases a triangle 0.900" (≈29⁄32") across its base with the base on the table side of the cut. Check: equal sides 0.900" (≈29⁄32"), apex 60.0 degrees.
    • Rip every bar of a shape in one session without touching the saw setup, and rip 10% spares. Dry-fit the first few bars into a unit or a short row before ripping the rest: half a degree of bevel error doubles at every joint and opens gaps across a row.
    See sheet 03
    DON'Ttouch the blade tilt or the thin-rip jig partway through a shape. Bars from two setups never meet cleanly; rip every bar of a shape, plus spares, in one run.
  3. 3

    Glue the logs

    • Log L: glue 18 bars side by side as drawn; make 1 log, 22.500" (≈221⁄2") long, for 11 slices plus 1 spare.
    • Log M: glue 18 bars side by side as drawn; make 1 log, 20.750" (≈203⁄4") long, for 10 slices plus 1 spare.
    • Glue on a flat, waxed or papered surface with cauls across the faces so the bars stay in plane; a log that is twisted gives slices that won't sit flat together.
    • Scrape the glue off, then square one end of each log.
    See sheet 04
  4. 4

    Crosscut the slices

    • Before cutting, draw a pencil V across the top of each log: every slice keeps a mark that says which way is up and where it came from.
    • On a crosscut sled with a stop block, cut slices 1.625" (≈15⁄8") thick (finished 11⁄2" plus 1⁄8" to flatten). Hold the off-cut side with a hold-down, never the fence side.
    • Cut 23 slices in all; stack them in order.
    • Logs without two flat adjacent faces rock on the sled: fix them to a straight plywood carrier with double-sided tape or hot glue blocks first.
  5. 5

    Glue the board

    • Lay out the 21 slices as numbered in the layout drawing. Every slice goes down as cut. Dry-fit the whole board and look at it before any glue.
    • Glue in stages of two to four slices, then glue the stages together. End grain drinks glue: brush a thin coat on both faces, wait two minutes, add a second coat, then clamp with moderate pressure and cauls top and bottom so the slices stay flush (the faces are end grain and are hard to plane flat).
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue all the slices at once. With more than four joints the slices creep out of plane under clamp pressure, and end grain is slow to flatten.
  6. 6

    Trim and flatten

    • Trim to 12.600" (≈1219⁄32") x 16.368" (≈163⁄8") on the red lines (crosscut sled or track saw, with a backer to stop breakout).
    • Flatten both faces with a router sled or a drum sander. Do not run end grain through a thickness planer: it blows out the trailing edge and can throw pieces.
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let dry, sand 220.
    See sheet 01
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain blows out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  7. 7

    Edges, feet and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1⁄8" chamfer and break the corners: end-grain edges chip if left sharp.
    • Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners (feet let the board dry evenly underneath).
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (end grain takes a lot: several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
