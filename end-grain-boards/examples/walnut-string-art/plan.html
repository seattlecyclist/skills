Overview

A big walnut end-grain board in feathered rows, two staggered lines of small maple triangles across it, and seven thin maple lines fanning out of one corner like string art
23-5/8" × 16-1/2" × 1-1/2"
21 of 21 checks pass

How it goes together

  • The field is one wood. A plank 19-1/2" wide is glued from two zones of walnut, with a small corner of maple let in along the joint, and crosscut into 27 rows, each 7/8" wide on the face.
  • Every other row is rolled over and slid 2-1/2". Rolling mirrors the slant of the growth rings, which makes the feathers. Sliding splits the joint into two lines, which makes the two staggered lines of maple triangles and the band between them.
  • What repeats: one plank makes every row. There are two kinds of row and they are the same slice, as cut and rolled.
  • The 7 lines are added last. Each is one saw cut right through the slab with a maple strip glued in that is exactly as thick as the kerf, so nothing moves and the rows still line up across every line.
  • The base board has no border, like the maker’s: the field and the lines run to the edge.

Wood to buy

  • Black walnut (upper zone of the plank): 6 bd ft
  • Black walnut (lower zone of the plank): 7 bd ft
  • Hard maple (the stick glued onto the chamfer): 1 bd ft
  • Hard maple (ripped into 9 strips 1/8" thick (7 lines and 2 spare)): 1.5 bd ft

Tools

  • Jointer and planer for the plank; table saw with a riving knife and a 45° tilt
  • Crosscut sled that takes a 19-1/2" plank, with a stop block
  • Track saw, or a carrier sled for the table saw, to cut the slab on the lines
  • Framing square, long straightedge, calipers
  • Flat board and two straight battens for the corner jig, packing tape
  • Bar clamps, cauls, Titebond III
  • Router sled to flatten the board, random-orbit sander
  • Mineral oil and board butter

Board feet include 25% for defects and milling. The two walnut zones are listed apart because they are picked for different looks, not because they are different woods. The plank is long: 53-1/4". If that is awkward, make it as two planks half as long, glued and cut the same way; keep the rows of each in order.

The woods

  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood. Softer than maple, so it shows knife marks a little sooner; the usual partner for maple in a checker.
  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.

The maker’s description names walnut and maple. The feather figure is not something the plan can guarantee: it comes from the growth rings of the boards you pick. Rings on a slant give feathers, flat arcs give arches. Either looks good; only slanted rings look like the photo.

The pattern

Read from the screenshot and the video’s cover photo
27 rows · 7 lines

What the photos show

  • A walnut end-grain board about 24 by 16-1/2 inches, in narrow rows that run the short way, a little under an inch wide. I counted about 27.
  • Feather figure down the rows: the growth rings slant one way in one row and the other way in the next. Neighbouring rows are the same slice, one rolled over.
  • Two level lines of small pale triangles, about 2-1/2 inches apart, a little above the middle. The triangles of the upper line sit in every other row and those of the lower line in the rows between. Above the upper line the wood is lighter and more figured, below the lower line darker and plainer, and between the lines the rows alternate.
  • That is what one joint in the plank looks like when every other row is slid 2-1/2 inches: one joint, seen at two heights.
  • Seven pale lines fanning from the lower left corner. Each runs from a mark on the left edge to a mark on the bottom edge, with the marks evenly spaced on both edges: the first line from the top left corner to the first bottom mark, the last from the lowest left mark to the bottom right corner.
  • On the cover photo the pale lines carry on down the edge of the board, so they go right through it. They are not a shallow routed inlay.

Where this plan is my reading, not the maker’s

  • The pale triangles may simply be sapwood on the corner of one of his walnut boards. I have made them a deliberate maple corner so the plan does not depend on finding such a board.
  • Whether he rolled and slid the rows, or turned them some other way, cannot be read from the stills. Rolling and sliding reproduces what the photo shows.
  • Row width 7/8", slide 2-1/2", triangle size and line width 1/8" are measured off the screenshot, good to about an eighth of an inch.

Why it stays in line

  • The strip is as thick as the kerf it fills (1/8" here), so each cut takes out exactly what its strip puts back. The slab stays the same size and no row shifts.
  • Every line runs from the left edge to the bottom edge, so every cut frees a corner piece that sits against both battens of a square corner. The large piece bears on at least one batten and on the strip. With the strip as thick as the kerf, that leaves it only one place to be.
  • Clamps close a joint best when they pull square across it. Save the wedge-shaped offcuts from trimming the slab: taped to the far edges they give the clamp heads a seat square to the cut.
  • Lines cross because later cuts pass through earlier strips. Neighbouring lines cross at 7 degrees or more, leaving long thin points of walnut between them; those are glued on both sides before the next cut is made.
  • Cut order is 1 to 7. Any order works; the last one cut is the one that looks unbroken.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
11 steps · 10 glue-ups
  1. 1

    Pick and mill the two zones

    • The plank is two zones of walnut, edge to edge: an upper zone 8-7/8" wide and a lower zone 10-5/8" wide, both 7/8" thick and 53-1/4" long. Each zone can be one wide board or two narrower ones edge glued.
    • Upper zone. Pick boards whose end shows the growth rings running on a slant. Those rings are what turn into feathers when every other row is rolled over. Lower zone: the darkest, plainest boards you have, so the two zones read apart.
    • Joint and plane everything to 7/8" in one session at one planer setting. The faces of this plank become the glue faces between rows, so thickness within 0.005" matters more than the exact number.
    • Rip the zones to width with both edges jointed square.
    See sheet 04
  2. 2
    GLUE-UP 1

    Let the accent corner into the upper zone

    • Mark the corner of the upper zone board where its top face meets the edge that will be glued to the lower zone. With the board flat on the saw and the blade at 45 degrees, rip that corner off so the chamfer measures 5/8" on the face and 5/8" on the edge. The offcut is a small triangle that falls away outside the blade.
    • Glue the maple stick, 1/2" x 1-1/8", flat onto the chamfer. It only has to cover the chamfer and stand proud both ways. Rub it into place, then hold it with a strip of tape every 3 inches pulled tight over the corner; a stick this small needs little pressure.
    • Next day joint the edge until it is square and all walnut again apart from the maple corner, then take a light pass off the face in the planer with the lower zone boards following at the same setting. The board is a rectangle again, with a maple triangle 5/8" by 5/8" in one corner.
    • A walnut board with a strip of pale sapwood along one corner gives the same triangle for nothing. That may be all the maker did; see sheet 08.
    See sheet 04
  3. 3
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the plank

    • Glue the upper zone to the lower zone, the maple corner on the joint. Clamp across the width every 6 inches with cauls above and below the joint so the faces stay flush.
    • The plank is 19-1/2" wide, too wide for most planers, which is why the zones were planed first. Scrape the squeeze-out next day and check the joint with a straightedge. A step you can feel has to come off with a hand plane or a sander on a flat block, because each face is a glue face.
    See sheet 04
  4. 4

    Crosscut 28 rows

    • Square one end of the plank on the crosscut sled, then set a stop and cut 28 rows 1-11/16" long: 27 for the board and 1 spare. Keep the last 2" of the plank as a handle and stop there.
    • Before cutting, draw a long pencil triangle across the top face of the plank. Number each row on that face as it comes off the saw. The triangle and the numbers keep the rows in order, which is what makes the grain flow from row to row.
    See sheet 04
  5. 5

    Lay the rows out: every other one rolled and slid

    • Stand the rows on end, end grain up, in number order, against a straight batten clamped to a flat board covered in packing tape.
    • Odd rows stay as cut. Roll each even row over so its other end is up, and slide it 2-1/2" along its length, away from the upper zone. A spacer block 2-1/2" long between the batten and each even row sets the slide.
    • Check before glue. The maple triangles sit on two lines 2-1/2" apart, each pointing up into the upper zone, the upper line's triangles on one side of their row and the lower line's on the other. The slanted rings should now meet in a V at every glue line.
    See sheet 04
    DON'TDo not turn a row end for end. Rolled over is the only move: it keeps the upper zone at the top of every row.
  6. 6
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue the slab

    • Glue the rows in 3 packs of about 9, each against the batten with its spacers, then glue the packs together. Small packs keep the open time short and the rows from creeping.
    • Clamp across the rows with cauls top and bottom. End grain drinks glue on the cut faces but these joints are long grain to long grain, so an ordinary spread is right.
    See sheet 04
  7. 7

    Flatten once and trim to size

    • Flatten both faces just enough to sit flat (sheet 07), leaving the slab as thick as you can.
    • Trim the two stepped ends square to the rows, 1/4" inside the shortest rows, to leave the slab 16-1/2" long. The odd rows give 6-1/8" of upper zone above their triangles. Trim the two long sides only if they need cleaning up.
    • The lower left corner, where the lines will fan out, has to be truly square: check it with a framing square. Every line is registered to those two edges.
    See sheet 04, 07
  8. 8

    Rip the maple strips to the saw kerf

    • Decide now which saw will cut the slab (track saw or table saw sled) and use that same blade from here on.
    • Cut a test kerf through one of the slab offcuts. Mill the maple to 1-3/4" thick and 25-1/2" long, then rip strips off its edge with the strip falling to the outside of the blade, adjusting until a strip slides into the test kerf by hand with no rattle. This plan assumes 1/8"; your blade decides.
    • Rip 9: 7 lines and 2 spare. A strip that is too thin shows as a dark glue line. A strip that is too thick pushes the slab apart and every row it crosses steps sideways.
    See sheet 05
  9. 9

    Mark the lines and build the corner jig

    • From the lower left corner, mark the left edge every 2-23/64" (2.357) and the bottom edge every 3-3/8". The last mark on each edge is the far corner.
    • Line 1 joins the top mark on the left edge to the first mark on the bottom edge. Each later line starts one mark lower and ends one mark further along. Draw all 7 in pencil; they are the centre lines of the saw cuts.
    • Corner jig. Two straight battens screwed to a flat board at exactly 90 degrees, faced with packing tape. The slab's lower left corner goes into it for every glue-up.
    See sheet 05
  10. 10
    GLUE-UP 4 to 10

    Cut and fill one line at a time

    • Line 1 first. Saw right through the slab on the pencil line, in one pass, with the slab clamped down on both sides of the cut. With a track saw, lay the rail on the line over a sacrificial sheet. On a table saw, fix the slab to a carrier sled with the line over the sled's kerf, as the maker appears to do.
    • Glue both sawn faces and both faces of a strip, set the strip in with a little standing proud top and bottom, and push the two pieces into the corner jig: the corner piece tight to both battens, the large piece tight to its batten and to the strip. Clamp across the cut, toward the jig's corner. Because the strip is as thick as the kerf, the slab's outer edges come back flush and every row is back in line; check the far edges with a straightedge before the glue grabs.
    • Leave it clamped an hour and let it cure four hours or more before the next cut. Then saw the strip ends flush with the edges and pare the proud strip level enough to sit flat.
    • Repeat for lines 2 to 7, in order. Each new cut passes through the strips already in; that is how the lines cross. The last line cut is the only one that reads unbroken end to end.
    See sheet 05
    DON'TNever make two cuts before gluing. Loose pieces cannot be put back in register, and small wedges of end grain break.
  11. 11

    Flatten, sand and finish

    • Flatten both faces to 1-1/2" (sheet 07). Sand 80 to 220, raise the grain with a damp cloth, and sand again at 220.
    • Ease the edges, add feet if you want them, then flood with mineral oil until it stops soaking in and finish with board butter.
    See sheet 07
07

Flattening & options

Two ways to flatten without a drum sander, the movement risk, and what can be changed
options

Route 1 · router sled and sander

  • Board on a flat base, shimmed so it cannot rock, between two straight rails; a wide straight or bowl bit in a router on a bridge.
  • Passes of 1/32 in. or less, the full face, then the other face.
  • Sand out the router marks from 80 grit. This is the route the plan assumes, for the first flattening and the last.

Route 2 · helical-head planer

  • The plank and its boards are long grain and go through the planer as usual. The slab is end grain and 23-5/8" wide, wider than most planers, so this route only applies to a narrower version of the board.
  • If the slab fits, follow George Vondriska’s method (sheet 08): sacrificial long-grain rails glued to the leading and trailing edges or a heavy chamfer on the trailing edge, 1/64 in. or less a pass, sharp inserts, and stand out of line.
  • After the lines are in, the cutters meet long-grain strips lying at an angle in end grain. I have no source for planing that and would use the router sled.

Wood movement: read before choosing the strips

  • An end-grain board swells and shrinks across its whole face with the seasons. A long-grain strip does not change length. Glued corner to corner across the slab, the 7 strips resist that movement.
  • Thin strips are what makers generally use for this kind of line, and the maker’s board looks that way, but I have no long-term report on a board like this one. The risk is a hairline crack beside a strip, or a strip end standing proud at the edge, after a few dry winters.
  • The safer version: make the strips end grain too. Crosscut 1-3/4" slices from the end of a wide maple board, plane a face, and rip 1/8" strips from the slice so the grain runs through the thickness like the rest of the board. They are short and fragile, so each line takes two or three pieces butted end to end in the kerf. Untested here.
  • Either way, keep the board oiled and never soak it.

Options

  • Size: "board": {"width": 18, "length": 12} makes a smaller board with fewer rows; the marks for the lines are recomputed.
  • Fewer or more lines: "params": {"lines": 5}.
  • Line width: set "params": {"line": ...} and "shop": {"kerf": ...} to your blade. A track saw kerf is nearer 3/32 in.
  • No accent corner to make: use a walnut board with a sapwood corner for the upper zone, or leave the triangles out; the two-tone band still shows.
  • Border: the maker’s board has none and neither does this plan. If you want one, make it end grain like the field, never a long-grain frame.
  • Juice groove or handles: rout them after the last flattening.
