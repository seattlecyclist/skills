01

Overview

3D tumbling blocks: a honeycomb of cubes seen corner-on, each cube a pale maple lid over a cherry left face and a walnut right face
11-7/16" × 14-3/4" × 1-1/2"
16 of 16 checks pass

How it goes together

  • Each cube is three diamond bars taped and glued into one hexagon log. The logs are sliced into hexagon pucks, the pucks are sanded and test-fitted, and all of them are glued at once inside a clamped frame, starting from the centre. This is how Tom Silva builds the board at This Old House. Expect a few hairline gaps; they are filled with glue and sanding dust before the last sanding.
  • Every cube is three 1.5" diamonds: lid, left face, right face.
  • Light lid, medium left, dark right reads as light from the top left; swap left and right to move the light.

The woods

  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
  • Cherry · Janka 950 · shrinkage 7.1%. Medium tone that darkens with light; soft side of acceptable.
  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood; a little soft, so keep it to accents or pair it with cherry.
03

Bars & rip setups

Every piece of the face is the end of one of these bars. Each drawing shows the section, then the table saw from the front for that rip
12 bars + spares · kerf 1/8"

The bevel rip

The sled. Every bevelled bar is cut on the rip sled (sheet 06, step 2), the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps that the builders in the best practices guide use. It runs against the rip fence, which stays on the side the blade leans away from: its usual place on a left-tilt saw; on a right-tilt saw move the fence to the left of the blade.

Two decks. The fence deck, between the fence and the blade, carries the locating fence and the toggle clamps. The landing deck, beyond the blade and under its lean, catches what comes off. Nothing is ever loose on the saw table.

Trapezoids and diamonds. They start as square-edged strips and are clamped to the fence deck for both bevels; only a waste sliver drops on the landing deck.

After every pass. Push the sled on until the work is clear of the blade, or switch off and wait for the blade to stop, before lifting anything out. Keep the riving knife in place.

The cards below. Each gives what differs for that bar: the strip to start from, how it is turned for the second bevel, and the figure to set the sled to.

1

60-degree diamond, 1-1/2" faces

blade 30.0° from vertical
  1. Mill the blank to 1.299" (≈1-5/16") thick (the bar's height lying on its 1-1/2" face).
  2. Blade square. Rip square-edged strips 2.349" (≈2-11/32") wide, the ordinary way, fence 2-11/32" from the blade.
  3. Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees from vertical; set it with a digital angle gauge, not the saw's scale. Cut on the rip sled as in The bevel rip at the top of this sheet; this bar's setup is drawn above. Clamp a strip against the locating fence and bevel one edge. Then roll the strip over (the same end leading, the other face up) so the sawn edge is against the locating fence, its sharp corner down on the deck, and bevel the other edge. The two bevels come out parallel. Set the locating fence with test strips until the face lying on the deck measures 1.536" (≈1-17/32") (1-1/2" plus the allowance); that one setting does both passes.
  4. Machine the two sawn faces: lay the bars on a sawn face, packed tight side by side between two fences on a flat carrier board (they lean on each other like roof tiles; tape across both ends), and plane or drum-sand to 1.299" (≈1-5/16"), half from each sawn face, which is the setting the blank was milled at.
  5. Check: corners 60 and 120 degrees, all four faces 1-1/2".

Reading the drawing

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
10 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (Hard maple, Cherry, Black walnut) and let them sit in the shop for a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained stock, quartersawn or rift where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
    See sheet 02
  2. 2

    Make the jigs

    • Rip sled. For every bevelled bar. It is the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps from the best practices guide. Start with a base of 1/2" plywood about 30" x 16", one long edge straight to run against the rip fence. Glue and screw a bridge of 3/4" x 4" hardwood, standing on edge, across each end. Tilt the blade to 30 degrees, raise it fully, set the rip fence 9" from it and push the sled through: the blade saws the base into two decks and notches both bridges, which are tall enough to stay whole above the cut and hold the decks together.
    • Fence deck. The 9" deck beside the rip fence. Screw a straight locating fence, 3/4" x 1-1/2", flat on it and parallel to the cut, through slotted holes so it can be reset for each bar shape, and mount three toggle clamps behind it with their pads reaching over it. The pads close on a caul, a straight strip 1/2" wide and 3/4" high laid along the top of the work, so the pressure is spread and no pad is near the blade. Lay the caul over the part of the work that has deck under it, toward the locating fence, never out over a leaning edge.
    • Landing deck. The other deck. Only waste slivers land on it. Leave the rip fence where it is for the whole run; every setting is made on the sled.
    • Sanding board. A flat offcut of MDF or melamine about 12" x 18" with a sheet of 120 grit paper stuck down with spray adhesive. The sides of the pucks are cleaned on it, and the sawn faces of the half pucks.
    • Glue-up base. A flat piece of melamine or MDF about 20" x 25", covered with packing tape so glue lets go. Stand it on two battens so the caul clamps can reach under it.
    • Frame boards. Four straight boards of 1x3, the frame Tom Silva clamps round his pucks. Two are 21" long for the long sides. Two are cut to 11.566" (≈11-9/16"), a hair under the width of the glue-up, so they fit between the long boards and push on the two end rows. Tape the inside edges. They lie flat on the base, loose, round the pucks, and stand lower than the pucks. Bar clamps go over the top of the pucks with their jaws on the outside edges of the frame boards: three across the width, one along the length down the middle.
    • Two cauls. Straight 2x2s with packing tape on one face, about 25" long, to hold the pucks flat while the frame closes. They lie lengthwise on the pucks, one each side of the long clamp, and are clamped down to the base at their ends, beyond the frame.
    • Crosscut sled. With a hold-down clamp, a stop block and a 1" spacer. Its base must be 1/2" or thinner: the log lies on one flat and stands 2-19/32" tall on it, and the blade, fully raised, clears 3-1/8". That leaves little to spare: measure your own blade's height above the sled base before the first cut. If it does not clear the log by 1/16", cut the pucks on a miter saw instead, as Silva does: log clamped to the saw's fence, stop block set once, the same 1" spacer.
  3. 3

    Mill the blanks and rip the bars

    • Joint one face and one edge of every blank, then plane to thickness: 1.299" (≈1-5/16") (material list, sheet 02). Set the planer with calipers, not a tape, and plane every blank of one thickness in the same session without moving the planer between them.
    • Crosscut the blanks to the lengths in the cut list. Then rip and true each bar shape as set out on sheet 03, which draws the saw and the rip sled from the front for every bevelled bar. Bars are ripped a little over and then machined to size, so the saw only has to be close.
    • Test the setup on scrap first: rip three short bars, true them, and tape them into a hexagon. The three must meet at the centre with no gap, and no light may show at the three outer joints. Silva and Dillen both tune the saw this way, by test cuts, until three pieces close. A quarter of a degree of bevel error leaves the hexagon three quarters of a degree short of closing; fix the tilt before cutting good wood.
    • The cut list has 12 bars plus 3 spares (one in ten, and at least one of every bar in every wood). Rip every bar of a shape in one run, spares included, then check a handful with calipers: all within 1/64".
    • Cut and true every diamond bar of all three woods in one session, at one saw setting and one planer setting. A hexagon log is never machined after it is glued, so the bars are the only place the fit is made. The maker lanwater ran short, cut a second batch at a new setup, and it did not match the first; that came back in the glue-up.
    See sheet 03
    DON'Trip a bevel with the work loose on the saw table, or with a bar between the tilted blade and the fence. Every bevel is cut with the work clamped to the rip sled.
  4. 4
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the hexagon logs

    • Log A. Glue bar 1 (maple), bar 1 (cherry), bar 1 (walnut) as drawn on sheet 04. Make 4 at 20-1/4" long, for 35 slices plus 5 spare.
    • Dry-fit first, with no glue: hold the three bars together so their wide corners meet at the centre, and look at the end. Mark one end of every bar before you start and keep the marked ends together. Seen from the marked end, every log has the same wood in the same place as on sheet 04: maple at the top, cherry and walnut below it. A log built the other way round has its cubes lit from the other side.
    • Roll a thin, even coat of Titebond III on one face of each of the three joints. Bring the bars together and wrap painter's tape tightly round the bundle every 3 inches along its length, as Silva does; thick rubber bands or band clamps do the same job. Tape pulls evenly toward the centre, where a bar clamp would make the bevels slide.
    • Check both ends before the glue grabs: three joints meeting at one point in the centre, the six outside corners sharp, nothing slid along its length. Wipe the squeeze-out and lay the log on a flat taped surface so it cures straight.
    • Glue the three spare bars into one more log in the same session: it replaces a log that measures out in the next step, and gives spare pucks. Silva unwraps after 30 minutes. Leave the logs overnight before sawing them. When the glue has cured, square both ends on the crosscut sled, taking only what cleans them.
    See sheet 04
    DON'Tclamp a hexagon bundle with bar clamps. Bevelled faces slide under a clamp; tape or bands pull every joint closed at once.
  5. 5

    Clean and check the hexagon logs

    • Scrape the dried glue off all six faces with a card scraper. The makers who build this way do not machine the log: its six faces are the planed faces of the bars. If you have a drum sander, one very light pass over each of the three pairs of flats, every log at the same setting, is this plan's own optional addition; no published build does it, so skip it if the logs already measure alike.
    • Check each log with calipers across all three pairs of flats, at both ends. Every reading should be 2.598" (≈2-19/32") and the logs should match each other within a few thousandths. A log that is out is set aside, and the spare log takes its place: its pucks would hold their neighbours apart.
    • This is the known weak point of the method. Nothing is machined between this glue-up and the board, so any error in the bars arrives at the board. Silva's own build ends by filling small gaps, and this plan keeps that step.
    DON'Tleave dried glue on a face. A bead a few thousandths high holds the next joint open.
  6. 6

    Crosscut the slices

    • Square the unmarked end of each log first. Lay the log on one flat with a corner touching the sled fence, marked end away from the stop, and clamp it down with the hold-down on its top flat. The squaring cut was made after the glue-up; repeat it only if the end is not clean.
    • Set the stop block 2-5/8" from the blade and use the 1" spacer: slide the log up to the spacer, clamp the log, take the spacer away, then cut. The slice comes off 1-5/8" thick (finished 1-1/2" plus 1/8" to flatten) with an inch of room beside it, so it cannot bind between blade and stop.
    • Cut 40 slices in all: 35 + 5 spare from log A. As each slice comes off, turn up the face that was toward the blade and pencil an X on it. Every puck goes into the board X up, so every cube is lit from the same side.
    • Hold the slice down on the sled base with a push stick as the blade comes through, hand well clear. Push the sled on until the slice is past the blade, stop, and lift the slice away before pulling the sled back.
    • The last 2" of each log is the part the clamp holds. It is never sliced: stop there.
    DON'Tcut a slice against a fixed stop with no spacer, or slice into the last 2 inches of a log. A trapped slice is thrown; a short stub cannot be held.
  7. 7

    Sand the pucks and make the half pucks

    • Sand the sides of each puck lightly with 120 grit, as Silva does: stand the puck on one side on the sanding board and take two or three light strokes, then the next side, the same count on all six. This takes off saw fuzz and any glue. It must not change the shape, so keep the puck flat on the board and stop as soon as the side is clean.
    • Set aside 7 good pucks for the half pucks along the two long edges (sheet 05 marks them with a half sign). Saw each one in two from point to point: the cut starts at the outer corner of the maple diamond, passes through the centre and runs out along the joint between the cherry and the walnut. Stand the puck on one flat on the band saw table, clamped in a wooden hand screw so fingers stay away from the blade, and saw just on the waste side of the line. Keep the half with the walnut for 4 of them and the half with the cherry for the other 3: sheet 05 shows which edge each goes on. Then rub the sawn face on the sanding board until the half measures 1.299" (≈1-5/16") from sawn face to the opposite flat, never more: a half that is over holds the frame board off the whole pucks beside it.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tsand a side until it looks perfect. Every stroke changes the hexagon; a few strokes clean it, a dozen make a gap.
  8. 8
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the pucks into the board

    • Dry-lay the whole board on the taped base from sheet 05: 7 rows, 28 whole pucks and 7 halves, every puck X up and turned so its maple diamond is at the top. The half pucks go at the ends of every other row with their sawn faces outward, so both long sides are straight.
    • Close the four frame boards round the pucks and snug the clamps with no glue. Look at every joint. Swap or turn pucks until the layout closes, then pencil a number on each puck, row by row, and lift them off in order.
    • Use a glue with a long open time: there are 35 pieces to place. Use Titebond II Extend, which Dillen chose for his board for this reason; ordinary Titebond III gives only about ten minutes. Work with a second person. Working alone, glue the board in two sessions instead: glue the rows of one half, leave the other half in the frame dry as packing with a strip of packing tape on the joint between the halves, clamp, and glue the second half the next day. That split is this plan's suggestion, not Silva's; he glues every puck at once.
    • Start at the centre of the board and work outward, as Silva does: brush a thin coat on the sides of each puck that meet pucks already down, and press it home. Keep the coat thin. Too much glue holds the joints open.
    • Close the frame boards and bring up the clamps a little at a time, across the width and along the length in turn, until every board bears. The long boards push on the straight sides. The end boards push on the points of the end rows, which seats each of those pucks into the V of the two behind it.
    • Before the clamps are tight, lay the two cauls lengthwise on the pucks, clamp their ends lightly down to the base, and tap any proud puck level with a mallet. Then tighten the frame clamps firmly, not hard, and wipe the squeeze-out.
    • Leave the board in the frame overnight and out of clamps a day before flattening.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Ttighten one direction fully before the other. The honeycomb locks, and a puck that has not seated cannot move afterwards.
  9. 9

    Flatten, fill, then trim

    • Flatten first, trim second. Flatten both faces with a drum sander (60 or 80 grit, light passes) or a router sled (wedge the board in a frame, spoilboard bit, passes of 1/32" or less, outside edges first), then a random-orbit sander; a router sled does the whole job if there is no drum sander. The slices were cut 1/8" thick of finished for this. Stop about 1/32" over 1-1/2"; the last pass after filling brings it to size.
    • Fill before the last pass. Silva lists this as a step: mix fine sanding dust from the board with glue to the thickness of peanut butter, pack it into any open joint, let it cure, then take one more light flattening pass over both faces, down to 1-1/2".
    • Then trim to 11.441" (≈11-7/16") x 14-3/4" with a track saw or on a crosscut sled. The trim lines are centred on the pattern (sheet 05 shows them against the slices): make the left cut 1/8" in from the left edge and the bottom cut 1/8" inside the deepest notch of the ragged bottom edge, square to each other, then cut the right and top edges parallel to them at the finished size. That leaves about 1/8" of waste on the right and 1/8" at the top. Tape the cut line and back the exit edge with scrap so the end grain does not chip.
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let it dry, then sand 220.
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain breaks out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  10. 10

    Edges and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1/8" chamfer and break the corners: sharp end-grain edges chip.
    • Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners, so the board dries evenly underneath.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
