Tumbling Blocks End-Grain Board

11-29/32" x 16-1/4" x 1-1/2" finished · 9 slices from 1 log · 18 bars · Hard maple, Cherry, Black walnut

  • 9 rows of 6 cubes; every cube is 3 rhombus bars (60/120 degrees, 1.25" sides) glued into a hexagon, hexagons glued side by side into a row log, row slices nested with a half-cube offset.
  • Light lid, medium left, dark right gives light from the top left; swap left and right to move the light.

Checks

Every check passes.

  • ✓ Species hard enough for a cutting surface (Janka 900+)
  • ✓ Species move alike (tangential shrinkage within 4 points) (7.1% to 9.9%)
  • ✓ Every species in the species table
  • ✓ Every bar is convex (a saw can cut it)
  • ✓ Bars do not overlap
  • ✓ Bars cover the whole finished board (no gaps)
  • ✓ Every bevel is 45 degrees or less from vertical
  • ✓ Board at least 1" thick (end grain under 1" can split) (1.5")
  • ✓ Board not too large for its thickness (11.9 x 16.2 x 1.5)

Build steps

1Buy and acclimate the lumber

  1. Buy the boards in the material list (Hard maple, Cherry, Black walnut) and let them sit in the shop a week, stickered.
  2. Pick straight-grained, quartersawn or rift stock for the bars where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.

2Rip the bars

  1. Joint and plane every blank flat and to its exact thickness (material list). Bars of one shape must come from blanks planed in the same session at the same setting, or they won't match.
  2. Bar A (60-degree rhombus, 1-1/4" side): Mill the blank to 1.083" (≈1-3/32") thick (the height of the 60-degree parallelogram). Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees from vertical (set it with a digital angle gauge, not the saw's scale). Rip the first bevel to establish the edge and discard the strip. Without changing the tilt, rip each bar off the same edge: 1.250" (≈1-1/4") from the blade at the table (a thin-rip jig against the blank's bevelled edge makes every bar identical). Each pass cuts the next bar's leading edge, so bars come off as parallelograms with no flipping. Check the first bar: 60.0 and 120.0 degree corners, every side 1.250" (≈1-1/4") on the slant.
  3. Rip every bar of a shape in one session without touching the saw setup, and rip 10% spares. Dry-fit the first few bars into a unit or a short row before ripping the rest: half a degree of bevel error doubles at every joint and opens gaps across a row.

3Glue the units

  1. Unit U1: glue 3 bars as drawn; make 6 x 19" long (for Log L). You can glue one long unit and crosscut it.
  2. Bevelled bars won't take bar clamps. Lay the bars face down edge to edge on packing tape in the drawn order, glue the bevels, fold them up so the tape pulls the joints closed, and wrap with more tape or rubber bands. Check each unit with the template from the diagram while the glue is wet.
  3. Let the glue (Titebond III, or II) cure overnight, then scrape the squeeze-out flush; the units' outside faces are glue faces for the next step.

4Glue the logs

  1. Log L: glue 6 units side by side as drawn; make 1 log, 19.000" (≈19") long, for 9 slices plus 1 spare.
  2. Glue on a flat, waxed or papered surface with cauls across the faces so the bars stay in plane; a log that is twisted gives slices that won't sit flat together.
  3. Scrape the glue off, then square one end of each log.

5Crosscut the slices

  1. Before cutting, draw a pencil V across the top of each log: every slice keeps a mark that says which way is up and where it came from.
  2. On a crosscut sled with a stop block, cut slices 1.625" (≈1-5/8") thick (finished 1-1/2" plus 1/8" to flatten). Hold the off-cut side with a hold-down, never the fence side.
  3. Cut 10 slices in all; stack them in order.
  4. Logs without two flat adjacent faces rock on the sled: fix them to a straight plywood carrier with double-sided tape or hot glue blocks first.

6Glue the board

  1. Lay out the 9 slices as numbered in the layout drawing. Every slice goes down as cut. Dry-fit the whole board and look at it before any glue.
  2. Glue in stages of two to four slices, then glue the stages together. End grain drinks glue: brush a thin coat on both faces, wait two minutes, add a second coat, then clamp with moderate pressure and cauls top and bottom so the slices stay flush (the faces are end grain and are hard to plane flat).
  3. The rows nest by their zigzags, so each row centres itself on the one below. Clamp across the rows with a little pressure from the ends too, or the rows can slide along the 60 degree faces.

7Trim and flatten

  1. Trim to 11.908" (≈11-29/32") x 16.250" (≈16-1/4") on the red lines (crosscut sled or track saw, with a backer to stop breakout).
  2. Flatten both faces with a router sled or a drum sander. Do not run end grain through a thickness planer: it blows out the trailing edge and can throw pieces.
  3. Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let dry, sand 220.

8Edges, feet and finish

  1. Ease every edge with a 1/8" chamfer and break the corners: end-grain edges chip if left sharp.
  2. Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners (feet let the board dry evenly underneath).
  3. Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (end grain takes a lot: several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.

Woods

  • Hard maple — Janka 1450, tangential shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
  • Cherry — Janka 950, tangential shrinkage 7.1%. Medium tone that darkens with light; soft side of acceptable.
  • Black walnut — Janka 1010, tangential shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood; a little soft, so keep it to accents or pair it with cherry.
