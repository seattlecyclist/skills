01

Overview

One stepped hexagonal pyramid seen from above and centred on the board, its terraces in maple, white oak and padauk by the way they face, with near-black wenge steps between
12-3/16" × 17-3/32" × 1-1/2"
22 of 24 checks pass

How it goes together

  • Bars are glued two at a time into small triangular logs in a V block. Every face of every log is machined flat, then the long logs are glued side by side into planks. A plank is one row of the board made long: it is planed, crosscut into rows, and the rows are glued into the board. No row is assembled from loose slices, and no glue-up has a face that cannot be machined.
  • What repeats: 4 bar shapes, 6 log types and 5 plank types make all 10 rows. Every row is a crosscut of a plank that is cut more than once, as it comes off the saw or turned.
  • Why: no row repeats another as cut on this board; a row rolled over is its mirror row about the line at row 5.5; so 5 plank types can make all 10 rows.
  • Concentric hexagonal terraces 1.5" wide round the middle of the board, parted by riser lines 0.75" wide; the terraces face six ways and are shaded in three woods.
  • One motif, not a repeat: the top half mirrors the bottom half, so each plank gives one row for each half.

Tools

  • Table saw, digital angle gauge (blade at 30°), rip sled with three toggle clamps
  • Jointer and planer for the blanks and bars
  • Drum sander or thickness planer (helical head is best) to true bars and logs in the V blocks
  • Crosscut sled with a stop block, spacer and hold-down; a track saw or the same sled for the final trim
  • Calipers and a straightedge
  • Shop-made V blocks, plank base and edge strips (step 2)
  • Bar clamps, taped cauls, Titebond III
  • Router sled or drum sander to flatten the board; never a planer on the finished end grain
  • Random-orbit sander, router or block plane for the edges, mineral oil and board butter

Board feet include 25% for defects and milling. Blanks are 5-1/2" wide after jointing and the last 1-1/2" of a triangle blank is not ripped (it is the part the clamps hold), so the bars per blank shown are what the blank really gives. Wider stock gives more.

The woods

  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
  • White oak · Janka 1350 · shrinkage 10.5%. Tyloses close the pores, so it is fine; red oak is not.
  • African padauk · Janka 1970 · shrinkage 5.2%. Orange-red; dust is an irritant, and it can bleed red into maple: wipe with solvent before glue.
  • Wenge · Janka 1630 · shrinkage 9.0%. Near black, but splinters and the splinters fester: avoid for working surfaces or use sparingly.
03

Bars & rip setups

Every piece of the face is the end of one of these bars. Each drawing shows the section, then the table saw from the front for that rip
130 bars + spares · kerf 1/8"

The bevel rip

The sled. Every bevelled bar is cut on the rip sled (sheet 06, step 2), the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps that the builders in the best practices guide use. It runs against the rip fence, which stays on the side the blade leans away from: its usual place on a left-tilt saw; on a right-tilt saw move the fence to the left of the blade.

Two decks. The fence deck, between the fence and the blade, carries the locating fence and the toggle clamps. The landing deck, beyond the blade and under its lean, catches what comes off. Nothing is ever loose on the saw table.

Trapezoids and diamonds. They start as square-edged strips and are clamped to the fence deck for both bevels; only a waste sliver drops on the landing deck.

Triangles. A triangle has no flat left for a clamp, so it is never the piece that is held. This is Vaughn McMillan's cut from Woodcraft Magazine's quilt-pattern board: bevel the edge of a wide board, turn the board over, and rip again so the triangle comes away as the offcut on the open side of the blade, never between blade and fence. McMillan runs a board at least 5" wide along the rip fence with a push block and lets the strip land on the outfeed table. Here the same wide blank is clamped to the fence deck instead, so no hand is near the blade, and the strip lands on the landing deck and rides out with the sled. The sliding fence with its spacer stack is this plan's addition to his cut: it sets the bar's width from behind the blank, so the landing side stays open as in McMillan's cut and the freed bar is never between the blade and a stop. No builder has tested it: cut test bars in scrap first.

After every pass. Push the sled on until the work is clear of the blade, or switch off and wait for the blade to stop, before lifting anything out. Keep the riving knife in place.

Truing the bars. Use the V block that step 2 on sheet 06 lists for this bar. The makers in the guide sand glued triangle logs this way on a drum sander; doing it to single bars follows the same idea but is not shown there. Take the first sawn face at one setting on every bar, then lower the head for the second face. With a planer only, truing single bars this way is this plan's own adaptation: prove it on two scrap bars first, feed several bars one behind the other, and if a bar chatters, true the bars by hand instead: 80 grit paper glued to a flat board, the bar in its V block, the same count of even strokes on every bar, checked with calipers. Do not skip the truing: the bars are ripped over size, and untrued bars make a log too big to close on its neighbours.

The cards below. Each gives what differs for that bar: the strip to start from, how it is turned for the second bevel, and the figure to set the sled to.

1

equilateral triangle, 3/4" faces

blade 30.0° from vertical
  1. Mill the blank to 0.681" (≈11/16") thick (the triangle's height plus the allowance).
  2. Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees from vertical (digital angle gauge). Cut on the rip sled as in The bevel rip at the top of this sheet; this bar's setup is drawn above. Clamp the blank on the fence deck with its free edge over the slot and rip one bevel along it; that strip is waste.
  3. For every bar: turn the blank over end for end (the same edge stays toward the blade, the other face goes down), push it back against the locating fence, close the clamps and push the sled through. The bar comes away on the open side of the blade and lies on the landing deck, base down and point up, on the sandpaper strip. Nothing stands beside it: it is never held between the blade and a stop. Push the sled on until the bar is past the blade, stop the saw, and lift it away.
  4. The bar's width is set from behind the blank. The locating fence and its clamps slide as one unit; between the fence and the fixed back rail goes a stack of spacer strips, each 0.537" (≈17/32") wide, which is what one bar takes off the blank. Set the first cut with test pieces until the bar's base measures 0.786" (≈25/32") as sawn; after that, each pass is: open the clamps, loosen the fence, add one spacer, push the fence up to the stack, tighten, clamp. Rip 7 spacers from scrap at one fence setting. Stop when less than 1-1/2" of blank is left; that strip is waste.
  5. True the two sawn faces in a V block: base against one wall, a sawn face up and level. Light passes, every bar at one setting, until all three faces are 3/4". How to true is at the top of this sheet.
  6. Check: 60 degrees at the point, all three faces 3/4".
2

60-degree trapezoid, 2-1/4" and 3/4" faces

blade 30.0° from vertical
  1. Mill the blank to 1.299" (≈1-5/16") thick (the trapezoid's height).
  2. Blade square. Rip square-edged strips 2.349" (≈2-11/32") wide, the ordinary way, fence 2-11/32" from the blade.
  3. Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees from vertical (digital angle gauge). Cut on the rip sled as in The bevel rip at the top of this sheet; this bar's setup is drawn above. Clamp a strip against the locating fence and bevel one edge. Turn the strip end for end, the same face up, sawn edge against the locating fence, and bevel the other edge. For this second pass only the sharp top corner of the sawn edge would touch a square fence, and a sharp corner crushes: rip a filler strip at the same tilt and stick it to the locating fence with double-sided tape, slanted face out, so the whole sawn edge bears on it. The bar sits wide face up, under the caul. The filler is a wedge as long as the strip and as high as the blank is thick, ripped at this same blade tilt so it tapers to a feather edge at the top; with it in place the fence line at the top face has not moved. Set the locating fence with test strips, both bevels cut, until the wide face measures 2.286" (≈2-9/32").
  4. True the two sawn faces in a V block: wide face against one wall, a sawn face up and level. One light pass on that face. Then roll the bar over so its wide face lies against the other wall: the sawn face just trued goes down into the V and the untouched sawn face comes up. One light pass on that face too; every bar at one setting, until the wide face is 2-1/4" and the narrow face 3/4". By calipers: the wide face is 2.286" off the saw, about 2.268" after the first sawn face and 2.250" after the second. How to true is at the top of this sheet.
  5. Check: 60 degree corners on the wide face, slanted faces 1-1/2".

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
11 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (Hard maple, White oak, African padauk, Wenge) and let them sit in the shop for a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained stock, quartersawn or rift where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
    See sheet 02
  2. 2

    Make the jigs

    • Rip sled. For every bevelled bar. It is the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps from the best practices guide. Start with a base of 1/2" plywood about 24" x 19", one long edge straight to run against the rip fence. Glue and screw a bridge of 3/4" x 4" hardwood, standing on edge, across each end. Tilt the blade to 30 degrees, raise it fully, set the rip fence 12" from it and push the sled through: the blade saws the base into two decks and notches both bridges, which are tall enough to stay whole above the cut and hold the decks together.
    • Fence deck. The 12" deck beside the rip fence (12" because it has to hold the back rail, the spacers, the 5" carriage and a 5-1/2" blank at once). The locating fence is a straight strip, 3/4" x 1-1/2", screwed along the front edge of a clamp board (3/4" plywood about 5" wide, 1" shorter than the space between the bridges) that carries three toggle clamps. Fence and clamps are one carriage: it lies flat on the deck parallel to the cut and is held by two 1/4" carriage bolts, one near each end, that come up through the deck (heads counterbored flush underneath so the sled still slides) and through slots cut across the clamp board, with a washer and wing nut on top. Cut a row of bolt holes in the deck about 2" apart across its width, so with the slots the carriage can be set anywhere on the deck for each bar shape, nudged by a tap and locked. For the first setting, tap it; for a change of a few thousandths, add a strip of masking tape to the face of the locating fence or to a spacer. For the bar rips set the blade one tooth higher than the work, not fully raised, and the pads always reach the same distance over the work. The pads close on a caul, a straight strip 1/2" wide and 3/4" high laid along the top of the work, so the pressure is spread and no pad is near the blade. Lay the caul over the part of the work that has deck under it, toward the locating fence, never out over a leaning edge.
    • Back rail, for triangle bars: a straight strip screwed along the rip-fence edge of the fence deck, parallel to the cut. The carriage is set off it with spacer strips that lie on the deck between the rail and the back edge of the clamp board (each bar card gives their width), one more for every bar cut, so the blank steps toward the blade by exactly one bar each pass. The rail is 3/4" wide and the slots in the clamp board are as long as one blank's whole travel (the spacer width times the bars one blank gives, plus 1/2"), so the bolts never have to move to other holes part way through a blank. The spacers only count from a fixed start. The start is the cut that takes the waste bevel off the edge of a fresh blank: when that position has been found by test cuts, fit a starter strip that just fills the gap between the rail and the back of the clamp board, and leave it there. Starter strip alone is the waste bevel; starter plus one spacer is bar 1; one more spacer for every bar after. If the bars come out wide or narrow, change the spacer width, not the start. Screw a small stop across the back end of both decks so neither the blank nor a freed bar can be pushed back along the sled, and switch off and let the blade stop before lifting a bar off the landing deck. Landing deck: the other deck. Stick a strip of 120 grit sandpaper along it where the bars land, and fix nothing else to it: a freed bar must have open deck beside it. Leave the rip fence where it is for the whole run; every setting is made on the sled.
    • V blocks. They hold every three-cornered part, for gluing and for truing. The sides of a block stop 3/16" under the top of the part: high enough to bear on nearly the whole part, low enough that the top stands proud for the caul or the sander. A V has no size except the height of its sides, so the blocks are named by that. Make a V block with 7/16" sides (3/4" MDF, ripped or planed to 7/16" thick; each side piece about 2" wide), for bar 1; a V block with 1-1/16" sides (2 layers of 3/4" MDF glued up, ripped or planed to 1-1/16" thick; each side piece about 2-1/4" wide), for bar 4; a V block with 1-3/4" sides (3 layers of 3/4" MDF glued up, ripped or planed to 1-3/4" thick; each side piece about 2-3/4" wide), for bar 2, bar 3, log A, log B, log C, log D, log E and log F.
    • To make a V block. Rip one edge of each side piece at 30 degrees from vertical, so the cut face leans back at 60 degrees. Glue and screw one side piece to a flat base (3/4" MDF). Stand a 60 degree drafting triangle on its point against that face (or dry-fit three test bars) and slide the second side piece up until its sharp lower edge meets the first. Fix this second side with screws through slotted holes, so it can be slackened: for gluing it is left loose and a clamp across the block closes it on the parts; for truing it is screwed tight against the drafting triangle. The two faces form a 60 degree V with no gap at the bottom.
    • Machine height. Under the drum sander or planer the tallest block and log stand 2-3/4" high: check your machine opens that far before starting. If it does not, true those logs in the block with a jack plane.
    • Block length. Make each gluing block 2" longer than the longest part it holds (the longest part is 14-1/2"). Line its V with packing tape so glue lets go, and stand it on two battens when gluing so clamp jaws reach under the base. Each log is an hour in the block. This board has 65 logs, so with one block that is 65 hours at the clamps: make 4 gluing blocks of the largest size, which brings it to about 17 sessions of an hour. Truing block: a separate block of each size, 6" longer than the part, with its V lined with 120 grit paper instead of tape so the part cannot slip, and a stop screwed across each end, 1/4" lower than the top of the part so the cutter clears it. The extra length holds a 3" scrap of the same section in front of and behind the part, so planer snipe falls on the scrap; cut the scraps from the spare bars and from one extra log glued for the purpose, and count that log into the bars you rip. Under a drum sander the gluing block with one stop at the trailing end will do.
    • Plank base. A flat piece of melamine or waxed MDF about 24" x 16", for gluing the logs side by side into planks. Stand it on two battens so clamps reach under it.
    • Three edge strips. Straight strips as long as the planks, as thick as the trued logs (about 1-29/32"; plane them to match a trued log) and 2" wide on the wide face, with one edge ripped at 30 degrees from vertical (the V block setting). Make them of straight scrap or of MDF glued up to thickness. Like the V block sides they are jig parts, wide and flat, and are ripped the ordinary way: wide face down on the saw table, square edge against the rip fence, fence on the side the blade leans away from. The rule that every bevel is cut on the rip sled is for the bars. The planks take 60 glue-ups of 45 minutes, one joint at a time, and a pair of strips serves one glue-up: make 2 pairs of gluing strips and as many bases, or allow 45 hours at the clamps. The outside faces of a plank section lean at 60 degrees; a strip lies against each, bevel to bevel, turned bevel down or bevel up as the face leans, and gives the clamps a square face. Tape the bevels so they do not get glued in. The third strip is screwed to the crosscut sled's fence, on both sides of the blade line, as the fixed face the plank bears on at the crosscut.
    • Four flat cauls. Straight 2x2s with packing tape on one face, a little longer than the planks are wide, to hold the logs down flat on the base.
    • Crosscut sled. With a hold-down clamp, a stop block and a 1" spacer. It must crosscut 18", the width of the widest plank plus the 2" edge strip on its fence, and cut square: check it with a five-cut test before the first row. Put the hold-down over the middle of the plank, on the side of the blade away from the stop.
  3. 3

    Mill the blanks and rip the bars

    • Joint one face and one edge of every blank, then plane to thickness: 0.649" (≈21/32"), 0.681" (≈11/16"), 1.299" (≈1-5/16"), 1.330" (≈1-11/32") (material list, sheet 02). Set the planer with calipers, not a tape, and plane every blank of one thickness in the same session without moving the planer between them.
    • Crosscut the blanks to the lengths in the cut list. Then rip and true each bar shape as set out on sheet 03, which draws the saw and the rip sled from the front for every bevelled bar. Bars are ripped a little over and then machined to size, so the saw only has to be close.
    • Test each setup on scrap first: rip three short bars, true them, and dry-fit them in their V block. Half a degree of bevel error shows as a gap you can slip paper into; fix the tilt before cutting good wood.
    • The cut list has 130 bars plus 16 spares (one in ten, and at least one of every bar in every wood). Rip every bar of a shape in one run, spares included, then check a handful with calipers: all within 1/64".
    • Wenge splinters, and the splinters fester: wear gloves when handling it rough, ease the arrises of every blank with a pass of sandpaper, and take lighter cuts in it than in the other woods.
    See sheet 03
    DON'Trip a bevel with the work loose on the saw table, or with a bar between the tilted blade and the fence. Every bevel is cut with the work clamped to the rip sled. Stop when a triangle blank is narrower than 1-1/2"; that last strip is waste.
  4. 4
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the triangle logs

    • Log A. Glue bar 1 (wenge), bar 2 (maple) as drawn on sheet 04, in the V block with 1-3/4" sides. Make 9 at 14" long.
    • Log B. Glue bar 3 (wenge), bar 4 (maple) as drawn on sheet 04, in the V block with 1-3/4" sides. Make 11 at 14" long.
    • Log C. Glue bar 3 (wenge), bar 4 (oak) as drawn on sheet 04, in the V block with 1-3/4" sides. Make 15 at 14" long.
    • Log D. Glue bar 1 (wenge), bar 2 (oak) as drawn on sheet 04, in the V block with 1-3/4" sides. Make 10 at 14" long.
    • Log E. Glue bar 3 (wenge), bar 4 (padauk) as drawn on sheet 04, in the V block with 1-3/4" sides. Make 11 at 14" long.
    • Log F. Glue bar 2 (padauk), bar 1 (wenge) as drawn on sheet 04, in the V block with 1-3/4" sides. Make 9 at 14" long.
    • Dry-fit first, with no glue: stack the bars in the block in the drawn order. The top must come out flat and level with nothing rocking, and the corners must meet at a point at both ends.
    • Spread a thin, even coat of Titebond III with a small roller on one face of each joint. Put painter's tape on the outside faces beside the joints so squeeze-out lifts off. Stack the parts in the block with its second side slack, lay a taped caul stick on the flat top (a little narrower than the top), and clamp down through the caul to the underside of the base every 4 to 5 inches, snug rather than hard. Then put a clamp across the block every 8 inches or so to close the loose side on the parts. The sides push the parts together while the caul pushes them down.
    • Check both ends before the glue grabs: corners meeting at a point, nothing slid along its length. Tap a part to line it up.
    • After an hour take the log out and scrape the squeeze-out while it is rubbery. Leave it overnight.
    • Mark every log as it comes out: look at the end that matches the sheet 04 drawing, and on that end grain pencil the log letter and a line along the top face (the face under the caul). That end is the marked end and that face is the reference face. Leave the ends of the log as they are: the plank's end is squared once, at the crosscut.
    • African padauk. Wipe every glue face of it with acetone or denatured alcohol just before the glue goes on, and let it flash off. Its colour and oil otherwise bleed into the paler wood beside it.
    See sheet 04
    DON'Tput more than four parts in one glue-up or skip the dry fit. Parts slide on wet glue; a stack that rocks dry will cure crooked.
  5. 5

    True the triangle logs

    • Every face of a log is a glue face later, so every face gets machined. Scribble pencil across the top face, set the log in the V block with 1-3/4" sides (second side screwed tight) and run it through the drum sander with 80 or 100 grit until the scribble just disappears; turn the log and do the other two faces.
    • No drum sander. A thickness planer can do this job, because these faces are long grain. The makers in the guide plane long-grain stock but sand their triangle logs, so carrying the V block through the planer is this plan's adaptation: glue one extra log from the spare bars and prove it on that first. Use the truing block from step 2: sandpaper-lined V, a stop at each end below the top of the log, a 3" scrap of the same section in front of and behind the log so planer snipe falls on the scrap. Passes of 1/64" or less. The other route with no drum sander is the router sled that flattens the board, run over the log in the same block clamped to the bench, passes of 1/32" or less; it is slower but nothing is fed through rollers. A helical head leaves the cleanest face; with straight knives feed so the grain runs downhill. With neither machine, clamp the block in the vise and use a jack plane and a straightedge.
    • Work in rounds. Every log through with its first face up at one drum setting, then every log with its second face up, then the third. The log sits a hair lower once a sanded face is against the block, so lower the drum by that hair at the start of each round until it just takes the pencil off; what matters is that every log gets the same setting in each round. That takes about 0.01" off a face: the logs glue up 2-1/4" on a side and finish about 2.215" (≈2-7/32"). The exact figure does not matter; that all the logs match within a few thousandths does. Sizes from here on are these finished sizes.
    • Check each log. The 60 degree triangle fits every corner, a straightedge shows no light along any face, and calipers read the same at both ends.
    DON'Tleave a glue face as it came out of the clamps. Dried glue and a part standing a few thousandths proud hold the next joint open.
  6. 6
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the logs into planks

    • A plank is one row of the board made long. The logs lie side by side, long grain to long grain, and every crosscut of the plank is a whole row, so no row is put together from loose slices. This board takes 5. Plank P1: make 1, 14" long, 13 logs across and 15-1/2" wide, for rows 1 and 10. Plank P2: make 1, 14" long, 13 logs across and 15-1/2" wide, for rows 2 and 9. Plank P3: make 1, 14" long, 13 logs across and 15-1/2" wide, for rows 3 and 8. Plank P4: make 1, 14" long, 13 logs across and 15-1/2" wide, for rows 4 and 7. Plank P5: make 1, 14" long, 13 logs across and 15-1/2" wide, for rows 5 and 6.
    • Why planks pay here. P1: Repeated 2 times: 1 row as cut and 1 row rolled over, far edge toward you. One glue-up gives all 2 rows. P2: Repeated 2 times: 1 row as cut and 1 row rolled over, far edge toward you. One glue-up gives all 2 rows. P3: Repeated 2 times: 1 row as cut and 1 row rolled over, far edge toward you. One glue-up gives all 2 rows. P4: Repeated 2 times: 1 row as cut and 1 row rolled over, far edge toward you. One glue-up gives all 2 rows. P5: Repeated 2 times: 1 row as cut and 1 row rolled over, far edge toward you. One glue-up gives all 2 rows.
    • Sheet 05 draws the end of each plank as you see it from the marked ends of the logs: which log lies where, which way up, and where its reference face is (the short heavy line). Every log has its marked end at the same end of the plank. Lay each plank out dry on the taped base and look at its end against the drawing before any glue goes on.
    • The glue faces are the trued faces of the logs. Roll a thin coat on one face of each joint.
    • Glue the logs in pairs first: one point down, one point up, one joint. A pair is a parallelogram, so its two outside faces lean the same way: one edge strip goes bevel down against the face that leans out over the base, the other bevel up under the face that overhangs, both taped. The joint leans at 60 degrees to the clamps, so part of the clamp force tries to slide one log up the other, and a bevel-down strip tries to climb. Stop both before any clamp goes across: clamp the bevel-down strip to the base at each end, and clamp a caul down across the top every 6 inches, snug, bearing on the logs and on both strips (the strips are the same thickness as the logs for this reason). Then clamp across the strips every 6 inches, through the middle of the logs' height, with moderate pressure. Do the first pair dry, with no glue, and look: corners of the logs meeting at both ends, no step along the top, strips lying still. This plank stage is the plan's own, so that dry clamp-up is the test of it. Forty-five minutes a pair in clamps.
    • Then glue pairs into fours and fours together the same way, one joint at a time: edge strips as the outside faces need them, cauls snug first, then clamps across. For this plank, counting logs from the left of the sheet 05 drawing: half 1 is logs 1 to 8: 4 pairs, then pairs joined two at a time; half 2 is logs 9 to 13: 2 pairs, then pairs joined two at a time, and log 13 added last as a single. A section with an even count of logs is a parallelogram and takes one strip each way, like a pair. A section with an odd count has both outside faces leaning the same way: both strips bevel down if its outside logs are point up, both bevel up if they are point down.
    • The plank is 15-1/2" wide, wider than a 12-1/2" planer, so it is built as two half planks (8 logs, about 9-31/32" wide, and 5 logs, about 6-21/32" wide). Leave the halves overnight, then true each half in the next step, both at the same planer setting, so they are the same thickness. They are joined at the end of that step, after planing.
    • African padauk. Wipe every glue face of it with acetone or denatured alcohol just before the glue goes on, and let it flash off. Its colour and oil otherwise bleed into the paler wood beside it.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue a whole plank in one go. More than three joints open at once slide on the glue before the clamps are on.
  7. 7

    True the planks

    • A plank built as two halves. True both halves here first, at one setting, then glue the halves together as step 6 describes and check the joint with a straightedge. The order is halves glued, halves trued, halves joined, joint checked.
    • Scrape the glue from both wide faces. These two faces are the glue faces between the rows of the board, so both get machined.
    • Scribble pencil across a face and pass the plank through the planer or under the drum sander, light passes, until the scribble just goes: about 0.01". Turn it over and do the other face. A plank is long grain from end to end, so the planer is safe here; feed it with the grain of most logs and take 1/64" or less a pass.
    • The plank starts about 1.919" (≈1-29/32") thick. Finish every plank at the same machine setting, so every row comes out the same width: about 1.899" (≈1-29/32").
    • Every plank is at least 14" long, longer than its rows need, because a planer cannot feed a shorter piece safely: the rollers lose it between them. The extra length is the price of planing the plank safely; it is waste or spare rows, and the material list counts it.
    • Joining the halves. Run the two gluing edge strips through the planer with the halves at that same last setting, so strips and halves are one thickness and the cauls bear on all of them. Then glue the two halves together on the flat base, an edge strip against each outside face as before, the bevel-down strip clamped to the base, with a caul clamped down each side of the joint and across it at both ends, so the planed faces stay in one plane. Check the joint on both faces with a straightedge. Planed halves glued on a flat base should show no step, and then the faces are finished. If there is a step, take it out with the router sled that flattens the board (or a drum sander wide enough for the plank), the lightest pass that clears pencil scribble from both sides of the joint; re-measure the plank and use that thickness for every plank.
    DON'Tleave one plank a pass thicker than the others. Rows of different widths walk the pattern out of line across the board.
  8. 8

    Crosscut the planks into rows

    • Lay the plank flat on the sled, the face that was up in the glue-up still up, marked ends of the logs to your left, away from the stop. Stand at the marked ends and look at the end of the plank: you see the sheet 05 drawing the right way up, with the sled fence on your right. So the edge where the red arrow starts on sheet 05 (the left of the drawing) is the edge away from the fence. One edge strip is screwed to the sled fence as a fixed face, with screws on both sides of the blade line, turned bevel down or bevel up to suit the plank edge that meets it, and that edge bears on it bevel to bevel; the blade cuts through the strip on the first pass and the strip stays put, so nothing loose is ever beside the blade. Clamp it down with the hold-down. Square the end nearest the stop (the unmarked end) first, taking only what cleans it.
    • Set the stop block 2-5/8" from the blade and use the 1" spacer: slide the plank up to the spacer, clamp the plank, take the spacer away, then cut. The row comes off 1-5/8" thick (finished 1-1/2" plus 1/8" to flatten) with an inch of room beside it, so it cannot bind between blade and stop.
    • Cut 15 rows in all: 2 + 1 spare from plank P1, 2 + 1 spare from plank P2, 2 + 1 spare from plank P3, 2 + 1 spare from plank P4, 2 + 1 spare from plank P5. As each row comes off, turn up the face that was toward the blade and pencil an arrow on it, starting at the end that was away from the fence and pointing along the row toward the fence end, with the plank's number beside it. Sheet 05 draws that arrow on every row, so you lay each row with its arrow pointing the way the drawing shows: solid when the arrow is face up, dashed when the row is turned over.
    • Hold the row down on the sled base with a push stick as the blade comes through, hand well clear. Push the sled on until the row is past the blade, stop, and lift the row away before pulling the sled back.
    • The last 2" of each plank is the part the clamp holds. It is never cut into rows: stop there.
    DON'Tcut a row against a fixed stop with no spacer, or cut into the last 2 inches of a plank. A trapped row is thrown; a short stub cannot be held.
  9. 9
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue the rows into the board

    • Lay the 10 rows out dry in the order on sheet 05, each with its pencil arrow pointing the way its red arrow is drawn. The table on sheet 05 says which rows go in as cut and which are rolled over, far edge toward you. Every row lies with its ends level with row 1: there is no slide on this board. Check that the corners of the logs meet corner to corner along the whole joint, as drawn. Truing took a few thousandths off every log, so points and strand edges land within about 1/64" rather than dead on; split any difference evenly along the joint. Then draw a pencil line across every joint so the rows go back in register under glue.
    • Glue two or three rows at a time, then glue those sections together. Clamp across the rows every 4 to 5 inches with moderate pressure, with taped cauls clamped across the top and bottom near each end, inside the ragged ends, to keep the rows in one plane.
    • Before any glue, take the saw fuzz off the sawn arrises of each row with one stroke of 220 paper on a block, and clamp a scrap block to the bench against the end of the first row so nothing can creep lengthwise.
    • The faces being glued are the machined faces of the planks, long grain: one thin rolled coat on one face of each joint. The pencil lines hold the register: check them after the clamps are on and tap a row back if it has crept.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery. Leave the board in clamps overnight and out of clamps a day before flattening.
    • African padauk. Wipe every glue face of it with acetone or denatured alcohol just before the glue goes on, and let it flash off. Its colour and oil otherwise bleed into the paler wood beside it.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue every row at once. More than three joints open at a time creep out of plane and out of register before the clamps are on.
  10. 10

    Flatten, then trim

    • Flatten first, trim second. Flatten both faces with a drum sander (60 or 80 grit, light passes) or a router sled (wedge the board in a frame, spoilboard bit, passes of 1/32" or less, outside edges first), then a random-orbit sander; a router sled does the whole job if there is no drum sander. The rows were cut 1/8" thick of finished for this. Stop at 1-1/2".
    • Then trim to 12.184" (≈12-3/16") x 17.087" (≈17-3/32") with a track saw or on the crosscut sled. The trim lines are centred on the pattern (sheet 05 shows them against the rows): make the left cut 9/16" inside the deepest notch of the ragged left edge (that is 1.662" (≈1-21/32") in from the left tip of row 1's long edge; mark it there with a square) and the bottom cut 15/16" in from the bottom edge, square to each other, then cut the right and top edges parallel to them at the finished size. That leaves about 9/16" of waste on the right and 15/16" at the top. Tape the cut line and back the exit edge with scrap so the end grain does not chip.
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let it dry, then sand 220.
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain breaks out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  11. 11

    Edges and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1/8" chamfer and break the corners: sharp end-grain edges chip.
    • Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners, so the board dries evenly underneath.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
