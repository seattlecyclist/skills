01

Overview

Interlocking three-armed pinwheels in maple, cherry and walnut that run into zigzag chains, every piece the same trapezoid
11-1/4" × 17-9/32" × 1-1/2"
24 of 24 checks pass

How it goes together

  • Everything stays a rectangle. Flat boards are glued face to face into a sandwich. The sandwich is ripped into slabs with the blade tilted, each slab is laid on a sawn face and its two points are ripped off square, and what is left is a rectangular bar with slanted stripes. The bars are glued side by side into a plank, the plank is planed and crosscut into rows, and the rows are glued into the board. Every glue-up is flat faces under ordinary clamps and every piece is machined flat; no piece is ever a triangle. This is how production shops build these patterns.
  • What repeats: 3 kinds of sandwich, 4 kinds of bar and 1 plank type make all 10 rows. Every row is a crosscut of a plank that is cut more than once, as it comes off the saw or turned.
  • Why: rows repeat every 1 row (slid along); a row turned end for end is its mirror row about the line at row 5.5; so 1 plank type can make all 10 rows.
  • One log, one bar: three trapezoids (1.5" and 0.75" faces) glued pinwheel fashion into a 2.25" triangle. No triangle bar anywhere.
  • The log is the one Mike Dillen documented after SPALM's board: trapezoid sticks in a V cradle, every face sanded flat. Dillen sliced his logs and glued the slices into rows; gluing the logs into a plank first and crosscutting whole rows is this plan's own step.

Wood to buy

  • Hard maple: 13.5 bd ft
  • Cherry: 8.5 bd ft
  • Black walnut: 19.5 bd ft

Tools

  • Table saw, digital angle gauge (blade at 30°), a riving knife or splitter, push blocks and a featherboard
  • Jointer and planer for the blanks and bars
  • Thickness planer (or drum sander) for the layers, the sandwiches, the slabs and the planks: every one is flat and long grain
  • Crosscut sled with a stop block, spacer and hold-down; a track saw or the same sled for the final trim
  • Calipers and a straightedge
  • Flat cauls, a taped plank base and an auxiliary rip fence that sits tight on the saw table (step 2). No V blocks, no bevel sled
  • Bar clamps, taped cauls, Titebond III
  • Router sled or drum sander to flatten the board; never a planer on the finished end grain
  • Random-orbit sander, router or block plane for the edges, mineral oil and board butter

The woods

  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
  • Cherry · Janka 950 · shrinkage 7.1%. Medium tone that darkens with light; soft side of acceptable.
  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood. Softer than maple, so it shows knife marks a little sooner; the usual partner for maple in a checker.

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
12 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (Hard maple, Cherry, Black walnut) and let them sit in the shop for a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained stock, quartersawn or rift where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
    See sheet 02
  2. 2

    Make the jigs

    • Flat cauls. Six straight 2x3s, jointed on one edge and taped on it, about 9" long, to clamp the sandwiches. Clamped in pairs, above and below, they keep the pressure even across the whole width.
    • Auxiliary rip fence. A straight board of 3/4" plywood or MDF, 4" high and as long as the rip fence, clamped to it with its lower edge tight on the saw table. The slabs have sharp edges; this fence leaves no gap for one to slide under.
    • Plank base. A flat piece of melamine or waxed MDF about 29" x 25", for gluing the bars side by side into planks. Stand it on two battens so clamps reach under it.
    • Four more cauls. Straight 2x2s with packing tape on one face, a little longer than the planks are wide, to hold the bars down flat on the base.
    • Crosscut sled. With a hold-down clamp, a stop block and a 1" spacer. It must crosscut 21-1/2", the width of the widest plank, and cut square: check it with a five-cut test before the first row.
    • That is all. This build has no V blocks, no bevel sled and no edge strips: every part is a rectangle or lies on a flat sawn face.
  3. 3

    Mill the layer boards

    • Sheet 02 lists the boards and sheet 03 the layers, one card for each thickness. Joint one face and one edge of every board, then plane to thickness.
    • An inside layer shows on the face of the board at its full thickness, so its thickness is a pattern size: 0.649" (≈21/32"). Set the planer with calipers and plane every inside layer of one thickness, in every wood, at the same setting. An outside layer is cut away at an angle by the squaring cuts, so it only has to be thick enough: the listed figure or a little over.
    • Where a layer is wider than one board, edge-glue boards to make it, then plane the panel. The joint is one wood glued to itself.
    • Plane both faces of every layer fresh on the day it is glued. Cut the layers of one sandwich to the same length and width.
    • Bar D is one solid wood (walnut), not a sandwich: rip and plane it to 0.650" (≈21/32") x 1-5/32", the size the striped bars finish at. Make 5 at 22-1/2" long.
    See sheet 02, 03
    DON'Tplane an inside layer by the tape measure. Its thickness is a stripe on the board; two layers a few thousandths apart show as a step in the pattern.
  4. 4
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the sandwiches

    • A sandwich is the layers glued face to face, flat. Sandwich S1: maple 1", walnut 11/32" (bottom to top), 4-3/4" wide and 1-3/8" thick. Make 5 at 23" long. Sandwich S2: maple 11/32", cherry 21/32", walnut 11/32" (bottom to top), 4-3/4" wide and 1-3/8" thick. Make 5 at 23" long. Sandwich S3: walnut 11/32", cherry 21/32", walnut 11/32" (bottom to top), 4-3/4" wide and 1-3/8" thick. Make 2 at 23" long.
    • Roll a thin, even coat of Titebond III on one face of each joint. Stack the layers on two cauls, edges and ends flush, and rub each one down to grab.
    • Clamp cauls in pairs, one above and one below, every 6 inches, snug in the middle first and then outward, with moderate pressure. Before the last turn, tap the layers flush at one long edge and check the ends: wet layers slide on each other. A brad or two in the waste at each end stops it.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery. Leave the sandwiches in clamps two hours and out of clamps overnight.
    • Joint one long edge of each sandwich square to its faces: that edge runs against the rip fence. Pencil the sandwich's number on one end; that is the marked end of every slab and bar cut from it.
    See sheet 04
    DON'Tglue more than three joints in one go. A tall wet stack skates out of line before the clamps are on; glue a five-layer sandwich in two sessions.
  5. 5

    Rip the sandwiches into slabs

    • Sandwich S1: blade tilted 30 degrees from vertical, checked with a digital angle gauge and a test cut. Each slab is 0.681" (≈11/16") thick, measured square across its sawn faces (1/32" over, for planing). The fence moves 0.930" (≈15/16") toward the blade between cuts. One sandwich gives 3 slabs; cut 11 + 4 spare in all.
    • Sandwich S2: blade tilted 30 degrees from vertical, checked with a digital angle gauge and a test cut. Each slab is 0.681" (≈11/16") thick, measured square across its sawn faces (1/32" over, for planing). The fence moves 0.930" (≈15/16") toward the blade between cuts. One sandwich gives 3 slabs; cut 11 + 4 spare in all.
    • Sandwich S3: blade tilted 30 degrees from vertical, checked with a digital angle gauge and a test cut. Each slab is 0.681" (≈11/16") thick, measured square across its sawn faces (1/32" over, for planing). The fence moves 0.930" (≈15/16") toward the blade between cuts. One sandwich gives 3 slabs; cut 5 + 1 spare in all.
    • The sandwich lies flat on the table with its jointed edge against the rip fence, and the fence on the side the blade leans away from, so the blade never leans over the wood between itself and the fence. The slab comes off the open side of the blade and lies free on the table; the part against the fence is the part you hold, and it gets narrower with each cut.
    • First cut. Set the fence so the blade takes a wedge off the open edge and leaves that edge a clean slanted face from top to bottom. Then move the fence toward the blade by the figure above and cut the first slab. Check its thickness with calipers, square across the two sawn faces, and correct the fence before going on.
    • The cut is deep. The thickest sandwich is 1-3/8" and the blade is close to its full height. Use a sharp rip blade, the riving knife or a splitter, a featherboard ahead of the blade, and push blocks. Feed steadily. If the saw labours, make each cut in two passes, the first at half height.
    • Stop when the top face of the part against the fence is less than 1/2" wide. That last piece is waste; so is the first wedge. Both are counted in the material list.
    • The pictures this method comes from show the slabs and the finished bars, not the saw setup. The fence side, the order of cuts and the stopping point above are this plan's working out. Rip a short offcut of a sandwich first and check the setup feels safe on your saw.
    • Plane each slab on its two sawn faces to 0.650" (≈21/32"), a light pass a face. The slab lies flat on a sawn face; the planer is safe here because the slab is long grain. Plane every slab of the board at the same setting, without moving the handwheel for the last pass: this thickness is the width of a bar, and it is a pattern size.
    • Stack the slabs marked end to marked end. On each sawn face the glue lines now show as straight stripes.
    See sheet 04
    DON'Tput the sandwich between a blade that leans toward the fence and the fence. A slab trapped under a leaning blade is thrown.
  6. 6

    Square the slabs into bars

    • Blade back to square; check it. Clamp the auxiliary fence to the rip fence, tight on the table. A slab lies on a sawn face and its two long edges slant: they are the old faces of the sandwich. Both slanted points come off square.
    • First cut. Lay the slab with the sharp corner of one slanted edge down on the table and against the auxiliary fence. Mark the cut on the upper face at the far edge, as given below for each bar, set the fence to the mark and rip the far point off.
    • Second cut. Turn the slab round so the fresh square edge is against the fence and rip the other point off. Set the fence 1/32" over the bar's size; the planks are planed to size later, half off each face.
    • Bar A (from sandwich S1): the first cut is 3/4" outside the nearest glue line on the upper face. Second cut 1-5/32" from the first. Check against the drawing on sheet 04: measured from the first cut edge, upper face, glue lines at 3/4"; lower face, glue lines at the edge. Make 11 at 22-1/2" long.
    • Bar B (from sandwich S2): the first cut goes exactly through the point where a glue line meets the upper face. Second cut 1-5/32" from the first. Check against the drawing on sheet 04: measured from the first cut edge, upper face, glue lines at the edge and 3/4"; lower face, glue lines at 3/8" and the edge. Make 11 at 22-1/2" long.
    • Bar C (from sandwich S3): the first cut goes exactly through the point where a glue line meets the upper face. Second cut 1-5/32" from the first. Check against the drawing on sheet 04: measured from the first cut edge, upper face, glue lines at the edge and 3/4"; lower face, glue lines at 3/8" and the edge. Make 5 at 22-1/2" long.
    • Measure from the glue lines, never from the slanted edges: the glue lines are the pattern. Set up the first bar of each kind with a marking knife and a square, cut it, and hold its end against the sheet 04 drawing before cutting the rest.
    • As each bar comes off, hold its marked end toward you and turn it until the end matches its drawing on sheet 04. Pencil the bar's letter on that end, and a line along the face that is then underneath. Sheet 05 shows that line as the short heavy line on every bar.
    • As with the tilted rip, the squaring setup is this plan's working out; the source shows only the result. Try it on a spare slab.
    See sheet 04
    DON'Tmeasure a squaring cut from the slanted edge. The edge is wherever the sandwich's outside face happened to be; the glue line is where the pattern is.
  7. 7
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the bars into planks

    • A plank is one row of the board made long. The bars lie side by side, long grain to long grain, and every crosscut of the plank is a whole row, so no row is put together from loose slices. This board takes 1. Plank P1: make 1, 22-1/2" long, 32 bars across and 20-25/32" wide, for rows 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10.
    • Why planks pay here. P1: Repeated 10 times: 10 rows as cut, some slid along. One glue-up gives all 10 rows.
    • Sheet 05 draws the end of each plank as you see it from the marked ends of the bars: which bar lies where, which way up, and where its reference face is (the short heavy line). A letter with a prime (A′) is a bar turned end for end, its marked end at the far end of the plank. Lay each plank out dry on the taped base and look at its end against the drawing before any glue goes on.
    • The glue faces are the planed faces of the bars. Roll a thin coat on one face of each joint.
    • Glue in sections of three or four bars. Lay them on the base in the order drawn, each with its pencil line where sheet 05 shows it, clamp across every 6 inches with moderate pressure, then clamp two taped cauls down across the top, to the base, so no bar rides up. The bars are square, so they clamp like any panel. Before tightening, sight across both ends: the stripes of one bar must meet the stripes of the next where the drawing shows them meeting, and the marked ends must be flush.
    • Then glue the sections to each other the same way until the plank is whole.
    • The plank is 20-25/32" wide, wider than a 12-1/2" planer, so it is built as two half planks (16 bars, about 10-13/32" wide, and 16 bars, about 10-13/32" wide). Leave the halves overnight, then true each half in the next step, both at the same planer setting, so they are the same thickness. Then glue the two halves together on the flat base with a caul clamped down each side of the joint and across it at both ends, so the planed faces stay in one plane. Check the joint on both faces with a straightedge. Planed halves glued on a flat base should show no step, and then the faces are finished. If there is a step, take it out on a drum sander wide enough for the plank, or with the router sled that flattens the board, the lightest pass that clears pencil scribble from both sides of the joint; re-measure the plank and use that thickness for every plank.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue a whole plank in one go. More than three joints open at once slide on the glue before the clamps are on.
  8. 8

    True the planks

    • A plank built as two halves. True both halves here first, at one setting, then glue the halves together as step 7 describes and check the joint with a straightedge. The order is halves glued, halves trued, halves joined, joint checked.
    • Scrape the glue from both wide faces. These two faces are the glue faces between the rows of the board, so both get machined.
    • Scribble pencil across a face and pass the plank through the planer or under the drum sander, light passes, until the scribble just goes: about 0.01". Turn it over and do the other face. A plank is long grain from end to end, so the planer is safe here; feed it with the grain of most bars and take 1/64" or less a pass.
    • The plank starts about 1.145" (≈1-5/32") thick. Finish every plank at the same machine setting, so every row comes out the same width: about 1-1/8".
    DON'Tleave one plank a pass thicker than the others. Rows of different widths walk the pattern out of line across the board.
  9. 9

    Crosscut the planks into rows

    • Lay the plank flat on the sled, the face that was up in the glue-up still up, marked ends of the bars to your left, away from the stop. Stand at the marked ends and look at the end of the plank: you see the sheet 05 drawing the right way up, with the sled fence on your right. So the edge where the red arrow starts on sheet 05 (the left of the drawing) is the edge away from the fence. Clamp it down with the hold-down. Square the end nearest the stop (the unmarked end) first, taking only what cleans it.
    • Set the stop block 2-5/8" from the blade and use the 1" spacer: slide the plank up to the spacer, clamp the plank, take the spacer away, then cut. The row comes off 1-5/8" thick (finished 1-1/2" plus 1/8" to flatten) with an inch of room beside it, so it cannot bind between blade and stop.
    • Cut 11 rows in all: 10 + 1 spare from plank P1. As each row comes off, turn up the face that was toward the blade and pencil an arrow on it, starting at the end that was away from the fence and pointing along the row toward the fence end, with the plank's number beside it. Sheet 05 draws that arrow on every row, so you lay each row with its arrow pointing the way the drawing shows.
    • Hold the row down on the sled base with a push stick as the blade comes through, hand well clear. Push the sled on until the row is past the blade, stop, and lift the row away before pulling the sled back.
    • The last 2" of each plank is the part the clamp holds. It is never cut into rows: stop there.
    DON'Tcut a row against a fixed stop with no spacer, or cut into the last 2 inches of a plank. A trapped row is thrown; a short stub cannot be held.
  10. 10
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue the rows into the board

    • Lay the 10 rows out dry in the order on sheet 05, each with its pencil arrow pointing the way its red arrow is drawn. Every row goes in as cut, number at the same side. Slide each row along its neighbour until the corners of the bars meet corner to corner along the whole joint, as drawn. The table on sheet 05 gives the slide as a figure: how far, and which way, the end of each row sits from the end of row 1. The figure is the starting point and the eye governs: truing took a few thousandths off every bar, so points and strand edges land within about 1/64" rather than dead on; split any difference evenly along the joint. Then draw a pencil line across every joint so the rows go back in register under glue.
    • Glue two or three rows at a time, then glue those sections together. Clamp across the rows every 4 to 5 inches with moderate pressure, with taped cauls clamped across the top and bottom near each end, inside the ragged ends, to keep the rows in one plane.
    • Before any glue, take the saw fuzz off the sawn arrises of each row with one stroke of 220 paper on a block, and clamp a scrap block to the bench against the end of the first row so nothing can creep lengthwise.
    • The faces being glued are the machined faces of the planks, long grain: one thin rolled coat on one face of each joint. The pencil lines hold the register: check them after the clamps are on and tap a row back if it has crept.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery. Leave the board in clamps overnight and out of clamps a day before flattening.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue every row at once. More than three joints open at a time creep out of plane and out of register before the clamps are on.
  11. 11

    Flatten, then trim

    • Flatten first, trim second. Flatten both faces with a drum sander (60 or 80 grit, light passes) or a router sled (wedge the board in a frame, spoilboard bit, passes of 1/32" or less, outside edges first), then a random-orbit sander; a router sled does the whole job if there is no drum sander. The rows were cut 1/8" thick of finished for this. Stop at 1-1/2".
    • Then trim to 11-1/4" x 17.287" (≈17-9/32") with a track saw or on the crosscut sled. The trim lines are centred on the pattern (sheet 05 shows them against the rows): The left edge is the planed face of an outside row and already on its line: leave it and measure from it. Make the bottom cut 25/32" in from the bottom edge. Keep the two square to each other, then cut the right and top edges parallel to them at the finished size, where the right edge is already a planed face on its line and 25/32" off the top.
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let it dry, then sand 220.
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain breaks out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  12. 12

    Edges and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1/8" chamfer and break the corners: sharp end-grain edges chip.
    • Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners, so the board dries evenly underneath.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
