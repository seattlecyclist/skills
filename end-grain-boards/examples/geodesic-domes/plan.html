Overview

A honeycomb of domes: each hexagon steps from a pale maple crown through a cherry ring to a full walnut rim, so every dome is a closed figure; a thin maple line and a walnut end-grain frame hold the field
12" × 13-13/32" × 1-1/2"
17 of 17 checks pass

How it goes together

  • Bars are glued two to four at a time into small triangular logs in a V block. Every face of every log is sanded flat, the logs are sliced, the triangular slices are glued into straight rows, and the rows are glued into the board. No glue-up ever has a face that cannot be machined.
  • Domes are hexagons 3 triangles to a side (54 facets, 4.50" across), shaded in 3 woods as if lit from the upper left.
  • Every bar is the same 0.75" equilateral triangle: one saw setup for the whole board.

The woods

  • Hard maple · Janka 1450 · shrinkage 9.9%. The standard board wood: hard, tight pores, pale.
  • Cherry · Janka 950 · shrinkage 7.1%. Medium tone that darkens with light; soft side of acceptable.
  • Black walnut · Janka 1010 · shrinkage 7.8%. Dark contrast wood; a little soft, so keep it to accents or pair it with cherry.
03

Bars & rip setups

Every piece of the face is the end of one of these bars. Each drawing shows the section, then the table saw from the front for that rip
18 bars + spares · kerf 1/8"

The bevel rip

The sled. Every bevelled bar is cut on the rip sled (sheet 06, step 2), the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps that the builders in the best practices guide use. It runs against the rip fence, which stays on the side the blade leans away from: its usual place on a left-tilt saw; on a right-tilt saw move the fence to the left of the blade.

Two decks. The fence deck, between the fence and the blade, carries the locating fence and the toggle clamps. The landing deck, beyond the blade and under its lean, catches what comes off. Nothing is ever loose on the saw table.

Trapezoids and diamonds. They start as square-edged strips and are clamped to the fence deck for both bevels; only a waste sliver drops on the landing deck.

Triangles. A triangle leaves no flat for a clamp, so the wide blank is clamped and the bar is cut free on the landing deck. The guide shows this sled for trapezoid strips only; the triangle pocket is this plan's own extension of it, so cut test bars in scrap first. It is not loose: the stop strip's edge is ripped at the same tilt and leans over the bar's sloping face, so the bar sits base down in a dovetail-shaped pocket between stop and deck, on stick-on sandpaper, and cannot lift, tip or creep. It rides along with the sled; nothing rubs it past the blade.

After every pass. Push the sled on until the work is clear of the blade, or switch off and wait for the blade to stop, before lifting anything out. Keep the riving knife in place.

The cards below. Each gives what differs for that bar: the strip to start from, how it is turned for the second bevel, and the figure to set the sled to.

1

60-degree trapezoid, 2-1/4" and 1-1/2" faces

blade 30.0° from vertical
  1. Mill the blank to 0.650" (≈21/32") thick (the trapezoid's height).
  2. Blade square. Rip square-edged strips 2.349" (≈2-11/32") wide, the ordinary way, fence 2-11/32" from the blade.
  3. Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees from vertical (digital angle gauge). Cut on the rip sled as in The bevel rip at the top of this sheet; this bar's setup is drawn above. Clamp a strip against the locating fence and bevel one edge. Turn the strip end for end, the same face up, sawn edge against the locating fence, and bevel the other edge. The bar sits wide face up, under the caul. Set the locating fence with test strips until the wide face measures 2.286" (≈2-9/32"); that one setting does both passes.
  4. True the two sawn faces in the 2-1/4" V block (sheet 06, step 2): wide face against one wall, a sawn face up and level. One light pass, turn the bar end for end, one more; every bar at one setting, until the wide face is 2-1/4" and the narrow face 1-1/2".
  5. Check: 60 degree corners on the wide face, slanted faces 3/4".

3

equilateral triangle, 3/4" faces

blade 30.0° from vertical
  1. Mill the blank to 0.681" (≈11/16") thick (the triangle's height plus the allowance).
  2. Tilt the blade 30.0 degrees from vertical (digital angle gauge). Cut on the rip sled as in The bevel rip at the top of this sheet; this bar's setup is drawn above. Clamp the blank on the fence deck with its free edge over the slot and rip one bevel along it; that strip is waste.
  3. For every bar: turn the blank over end for end (the same edge stays toward the blade, the other face goes down), slide it across until its sharp lower edge touches the landing stop, close the clamps on it and push the sled through. The bar is left on the landing deck, base down and point up, between the stop and the blade. Screw the landing stop with its sharp lower edge 0.786" (≈25/32") from the blade at the deck, then confirm on a test bar. Plane the stop strip to 1/2" thick, lower than this bar, so the blade passes over it. The blank's bevelled edge slides in under the stop's leaning face until the two faces meet. Back the blank for every pass: fill the gap between its rear edge and the locating fence with spacer strips ripped from scrap to suit (the gap grows by about one bar's width every pass, so keep the offcut strips and add one each time), so the clamps are not the only thing holding it in place. Stop when less than 1-1/2" of blank is left; that strip is waste.
  4. True the two sawn faces in the 3/4" V block (sheet 06, step 2): base against one wall, a sawn face up and level. Light passes, every bar at one setting, until all three faces are 3/4".
  5. Check: 60 degrees at the point, all three faces 3/4".

06

Building it

In order, with the reason where it isn't obvious
12 steps
  1. 1

    Buy and acclimate the lumber

    • Buy the boards in the material list (Hard maple, Cherry, Black walnut) and let them sit in the shop for a week, stickered.
    • Pick straight-grained stock, quartersawn or rift where you can: it moves less and the end grain shows tight, even rings.
    See sheet 02
  2. 2

    Make the jigs

    • Rip sled. For every bevelled bar. It is the sliding rip jig with toggle clamps from the best practices guide. Start with a base of 1/2" plywood about 34" x 16", one long edge straight to run against the rip fence. Glue and screw a bridge of 3/4" x 4" hardwood, standing on edge, across each end. Tilt the blade to 30 degrees, raise it fully, set the rip fence 9" from it and push the sled through: the blade saws the base into two decks and notches both bridges, which are tall enough to stay whole above the cut and hold the decks together.
    • Fence deck. The 9" deck beside the rip fence. Screw a straight locating fence, 3/4" x 1-1/2", flat on it and parallel to the cut, through slotted holes so it can be reset for each bar shape, and mount three toggle clamps behind it with their pads reaching over it. The pads close on a caul, a straight strip 1/2" wide and 3/4" high laid along the top of the work, so the pressure is spread and no pad is near the blade. Lay the caul over the part of the work that has deck under it, toward the locating fence, never out over a leaning edge.
    • Landing deck. The other deck. For triangle bars a stop strip is screwed to it parallel to the cut: a strip planed thinner than the bar (each bar card gives the thickness) with one edge ripped at the blade's tilt, set so that edge leans toward the blade and over the bar, with a strip of stick-on 120 grit sandpaper on the deck in front of it. Leave the rip fence where it is for the whole run; every setting is made on the sled.
    • V blocks. They hold every three-cornered part, for gluing and for truing. The sides of a block stop 3/16" under the top of the part: high enough to bear on nearly the whole part, low enough that the top stands proud for the caul or the sander. A V has no size except the height of its sides, so the blocks are named by that. Make a V block with 7/16" sides (3/4" MDF, ripped or planed to 7/16" thick; each side piece about 2" wide), for bar 3; a V block with 1-1/16" sides (2 layers of 3/4" MDF glued up, ripped or planed to 1-1/16" thick; each side piece about 2-1/4" wide), for bar 2; a V block with 1-3/4" sides (3 layers of 3/4" MDF glued up, ripped or planed to 1-3/4" thick; each side piece about 2-3/4" wide), for bar 1 and log A.
    • To make a V block. Rip one edge of each side piece at 30 degrees from vertical, so the cut face leans back at 60 degrees. Glue and screw one side piece to a flat base (3/4" MDF). Stand a 60 degree drafting triangle on its point against that face (or dry-fit three test bars) and slide the second side piece up until its sharp lower edge meets the first. Fix this second side with screws through slotted holes, so it can be slackened: for gluing it is left loose and a clamp across the block closes it on the parts; for truing it is screwed tight against the drafting triangle. The two faces form a 60 degree V with no gap at the bottom.
    • Machine height. Under the drum sander or planer the tallest block and log stand 2-3/4" high: check your machine opens that far before starting. If it does not, true those logs in the block with a jack plane.
    • Block length. Make each block 2" longer than the longest part it holds (the longest log is 24-1/4"). Line the V with packing tape so glue lets go, screw a stop across the end that goes into the machine last so the sander or planer cannot push the part out, and stand the block on two battens when gluing so clamp jaws reach under the base. A second block of the largest size lets you glue one log while another cures.
    • Row jig. A flat melamine or waxed MDF base about 20" x 9". Screw one straight fence along it and a cleat parallel to it about 4-1/4" away. The second fence is loose and sawn into short blocks, one for every slice that has its base on that side and one for the end block that sits on that side, each with its own wedge between it and the cleat. Driving or easing one wedge moves one slice without disturbing the others. Set the blocks to the slices themselves, never to a number.
    • Row truing carrier. A flat board with two straight, square rails screwed down 1-5/8" apart (a slice thick), about 1-1/2" high. A glued row stands on edge between the rails with its top edge proud, so the drum sander (or a helical-head planer, see the row truing step) can skim it.
    • Crosscut sled. With a stop block and a 1" spacer. Its base must be 1/2" or thinner: the tallest log stands 1-29/32" on it and the blade, fully raised, clears 3-1/8". Raise the blade and check it clears the top of the log before the first cut.
    • Sled cradle. For these three-cornered logs add a cradle to the sled, two strips with one edge ripped at 30 degrees (offcuts from the V blocks). Screw one to the sled base against the sled fence, sloping face toward the log; the log lies on one face on the sled base with a sloping face against that strip, and the second strip is pushed against its other sloping face and held by two bar clamps that reach across the sled fence and the strip. Both strips stop 1/4" short of the blade's path, so the slice comes off free of the cradle. The log is then held on all three faces (drawing at the crosscut step).
  3. 3

    Mill the blanks and rip the bars

    • Joint one face and one edge of every blank, then plane to thickness: 0.649" (≈21/32"), 0.681" (≈11/16") (material list, sheet 02). Set the planer with calipers, not a tape, and plane every blank of one thickness in the same session without moving the planer between them.
    • Crosscut the blanks to the lengths in the cut list. Then rip and true each bar shape as set out on sheet 03, which draws the saw and the rip sled from the front for every bevelled bar. Bars are ripped a little over and then machined to size, so the saw only has to be close.
    • Test each setup on scrap first: rip three short bars, true them, and dry-fit them in their V block. Half a degree of bevel error shows as a gap you can slip paper into; fix the tilt before cutting good wood.
    • The cut list has 18 bars plus 3 spares (one in ten, and at least one of every bar in every wood). Rip every bar of a shape in one run, spares included, then check a handful with calipers: all within 1/64".
    See sheet 03
    DON'Trip a bevel with the work loose on the saw table, or with a bar between the tilted blade and the fence. Every bevel is cut with the work clamped to the rip sled. Stop when a triangle blank is narrower than 1-1/2"; that last strip is waste.
  4. 4
    GLUE-UP 1

    Glue the triangle logs

    • Log A. Glue bar 1 (walnut), bar 2 (cherry), bar 3 (maple) as drawn on sheet 04, in the V block with 1-3/4" sides. Make 6 at 23-3/4" long, for 66 slices plus 6 spare.
    • Dry-fit first, with no glue: stack the bars in the block in the drawn order. The top must come out flat and level with nothing rocking, and the corners must meet at a point at both ends.
    • Spread a thin, even coat of Titebond III with a small roller on one face of each joint. Put painter's tape on the outside faces beside the joints so squeeze-out lifts off. Stack the parts in the block with its second side slack, lay a taped caul stick on the flat top (a little narrower than the top), and clamp down through the caul to the underside of the base every 4 to 5 inches, snug rather than hard. Then put a clamp across the block every 8 inches or so to close the loose side on the parts. The sides push the parts together while the caul pushes them down.
    • Check both ends before the glue grabs: corners meeting at a point, nothing slid along its length. Tap a part to line it up.
    • After an hour take the log out and scrape the squeeze-out while it is rubbery. Leave it overnight.
    • Mark every log as it comes out: look at the end that matches the sheet 04 drawing, and on that end grain pencil the log letter and a line along the top face (the face under the caul). That end is the marked end and that face is the reference face. When the glue has cured, square both ends (re-mark the end afterwards) on the crosscut sled, taking only what cleans them.
    See sheet 04
    DON'Tput more than four parts in one glue-up or skip the dry fit. Parts slide on wet glue; a stack that rocks dry will cure crooked.
  5. 5

    True the triangle logs

    • Every face of a log is a glue face later, so every face gets machined. Scribble pencil across the top face, set the log in the V block with 1-3/4" sides (second side screwed tight) and run it through the drum sander with 80 or 100 grit until the scribble just disappears; turn the log and do the other two faces.
    • No drum sander. A thickness planer can do this job, because these faces are long grain. The makers in the guide plane long-grain stock but sand their triangle logs, so carrying the V block through the planer is this plan's adaptation: prove it on a spare log first. Send the log through in the same V block, passes of 1/64" or less, the block's stop at the end that goes in last. A helical head leaves the cleanest face; with straight knives feed so the grain runs downhill. With neither machine, clamp the block in the vise and use a jack plane and a straightedge.
    • Work in rounds. Every log through with its first face up at one drum setting, then every log with its second face up, then the third. The log sits a hair lower once a sanded face is against the block, so lower the drum by that hair at the start of each round until it just takes the pencil off; what matters is that every log gets the same setting in each round. That takes about 0.01" off a face: the logs glue up 2-1/4" on a side and finish about 2.215" (≈2-7/32"). The exact figure does not matter; that all the logs match within a few thousandths does. Sizes from here on are these finished sizes.
    • Check each log. The 60 degree triangle fits every corner, a straightedge shows no light along any face, and calipers read the same at both ends.
    DON'Tleave a glue face as it came out of the clamps. Dried glue and a part standing a few thousandths proud hold the next joint open.
  6. 6

    Crosscut the slices

    • Square the unmarked end of each log first. Lay the log in the sled cradle on its reference face, marked end away from the stop. Push the second strip hard against the log and put two bar clamps across the sled fence and that strip, both on the side of the blade away from the stop (clamp jaws cannot reach under a sled, so the clamps squeeze fence, log and strip together above the base): the log is pinched between the two strips. Add a hold-down on the sled fence (a toggle clamp or a hand screw) pressing on the log's top edge through a scrap, so neither the log nor the loose strip can ride up. To move the log on, slacken one clamp, slide the log, tighten.
    • Set the stop block 2-5/8" from the blade and use the 1" spacer: slide the log up to the spacer, clamp the log, take the spacer away, then cut. The slice comes off 1-5/8" thick (finished 1-1/2" plus 1/8" to flatten) with an inch of room beside it, so it cannot bind between blade and stop.
    • Cut 72 slices in all: 66 + 6 spare from log A. As each slice comes off, turn up the face that was toward the blade and pencil a short line on it along the edge that lay on the sled. That line is the mark sheet 05 draws inside every slice. Stack the slices by letter.
    • Hold the slice down on the sled base with a push stick as the blade comes through, hand well clear. Push the sled on until the slice is past the blade, stop, and lift the slice away before pulling the sled back.
    • The last 2" of each log is the part the clamp holds. It is never sliced: stop there.
    DON'Tcut a slice against a fixed stop with no spacer, or slice into the last 2 inches of a log. A trapped slice is thrown; a short stub cannot be held.
  7. 7
    GLUE-UP 2

    Glue the slices into rows

    • Lay out the whole board dry on a flat surface from sheet 05: 6 rows of up to 11 slices. Each slice goes pencil line up, with the line on the edge where the drawing shows it. A letter with a prime (A′) is turned over, line underneath; the drawing still shows which edge the line is on. Look at the whole pattern before any glue goes on.
    • The glue faces here are the trued sides of the logs: long grain. Roll a thin coat on one face of each joint and keep glue off the end grain.
    • Cut a spare slice in half on the band saw, from one corner to the middle of the opposite side. The two halves are the end blocks: they turn the slanted ends of a row into square faces for the clamps.
    • Glue the slices in pairs first, in the row jig: slices 1 and 2 of the row, 3 and 4, and so on (an odd last slice waits). Two slices make a diamond. Set the pair between the two end blocks. One end block has its short edge on the fixed fence; the other has it on the loose side and gets a loose block and wedge behind it, like a slice, so the clamp cannot push it up the slope. Bring up the loose blocks, wedge them lightly, and put one clamp along the jig across the end blocks. Stick packing tape on the end blocks' slanted faces so they do not get glued in. Lay a taped caul on top with a weight. Thirty minutes a pair.
    • Then glue pairs into fours, and fours into the row, the same way: end blocks, wedged fence, one or two clamps along the row, caul and weight on top. Before tightening, tap each slice down flat and check its points meet its neighbours'; ease or drive the wedge beside any slice that needs it.
    • Each row comes out about 1.919" (≈1-29/32") wide before truing.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Ttrust a row that rocks in the jig. A slice that sits proud of the fence line will hold the next row off; take it out and find out why.
  8. 8

    True the rows

    • Scrape the glue from each row. Its two long edges are the glue faces for the board, and every one of them gets machined.
    • Stand the row on edge in the truing carrier, scribble pencil along the top edge, and pass it under the drum sander until the scribble just goes, about 0.01". Turn the row over and do the other edge. Every row gets the same passes at the same setting, so all rows finish the same width, about 1.899" (≈1-29/32").
    • No drum sander. The guide keeps the planer for stock that is still long grain, and a row's edge is cut across the grain. The route that stays inside the guide is by hand: glue 80 grit paper to a flat board, hold the row against a square block, and take the same number of even strokes on every edge. A helical-head planer is this plan's adaptation, not the guide's, and the risk is a broken-out last slice: only with the row in the carrier, a scrap block of the same height taped tight behind the trailing end, passes of 1/64" or less, and a test row first. Never with straight knives, never a row loose, and keep rows off the jointer.
  9. 9
    GLUE-UP 3

    Glue the rows into the board

    • Dry-fit the 6 rows in order. Slide each row along its neighbour until the corners of the slices meet corner to corner along the whole joint, then draw a pencil line across every joint so the rows go back in register under glue.
    • Glue two or three rows at a time, then glue those sections together. Clamp across the rows every 4 to 5 inches with moderate pressure, with taped cauls clamped across the top and bottom near each end, inside the ragged ends, to keep the rows in one plane.
    • The faces being glued are long grain (the sides of the logs): one thin rolled coat on one face of each joint. The pencil lines hold the register: check them after the clamps are on and tap a row back if it has crept.
    • Scrape the squeeze-out when rubbery. Leave the board in clamps overnight and out of clamps a day before flattening.
    See sheet 05
    DON'Tglue every row at once. More than three joints open at a time creep out of plane and out of register before the clamps are on.
  10. 10

    Flatten, then trim

    • Flatten first, trim second. Flatten both faces with a drum sander (60 or 80 grit, light passes) or a router sled (wedge the board in a frame, spoilboard bit, passes of 1/32" or less, outside edges first), then a random-orbit sander; a router sled does the whole job if there is no drum sander. the slices were cut 1/8" thick of finished for this. Stop as soon as both faces are clean and the board is an even thickness; leave it over 1-1/2", because it is flattened once more with its frame on.
    • Then trim to 9-3/4" x 11.141" (≈11-5/32") with a track saw or on a crosscut sled. The trim lines are centred on the pattern (sheet 05 shows them against the slices): make the left cut 21/32" inside the deepest notch of the ragged left edge and the bottom cut 1/8" in from the bottom edge, square to each other, then cut the right and top edges parallel to them at the finished size. That leaves about 21/32" of waste on the right and 1/8" at the top. Tape the cut line and back the exit edge with scrap so the end grain does not chip.
    DON'Trun the board through a thickness planer. End grain breaks out at the trailing edge and the planer can throw pieces.
  11. 11
    GLUE-UP 4

    Add the frame

    • Round the field goes a 3/8" line of Cherry and then a 3/4" frame of Black walnut. All of it stands on end like everything else, so it swells and shrinks with the field. A long-grain frame would split the board.
    • Making the strips. Plane the board to the thickness below with both edges jointed parallel. Crosscut blocks from it on the sled, each 1-5/8" long, with the same spacer stop and setting used for the slices; the board lies flat on the sled under a hold-down, and the last 2 inches are not cut. Stand the blocks on end and glue them edge to edge into four strips in the row jig: against the fixed fence, the loose blocks wedged up to them so the strip cannot buckle sideways, a taped caul with a weight on top, and one clamp along each strip with light pressure. These joints are long grain to long grain, and in one wood they disappear.
    • The line. Cherry, planed 3/8" thick. 13 blocks, each 3/8" x 5-1/4" seen from above: two strips of 3 for the long sides (at least 11-21/32" long), two of 3 for the ends (at least 11"), and one spare.
    • The frame. Black walnut, planed 3/4" thick. 13 blocks, each 3/4" x 5-1/4" seen from above: two strips of 3 for the long sides (at least 12-13/32" long), two of 3 for the ends (at least 12-1/2"), and one spare.
    • Scrape each strip and pass the face that meets the board under the drum sander once (or take a few strokes on the 80 grit sanding board), so it is flat across the block joints.
    • Work one ring at a time, the line first. Glue the two long strips to the long edges, overhanging both ends, with clamps across the board every 4 to 5 inches and a taped caul over each joint to keep the strips level with the field. When the glue has set, saw the overhanging ends flush with the short edges on the sled, taking the strip ends and no more than a shaving of the field.
    • Glue the two end strips across the ends the same way, clamping along the length of the board, then saw their overhang flush with the long sides. The outside of the line is the glue face for the frame, so true it before going on: skim all four edges of the board with a light trim cut on the sled or track saw, a shaving only, so each is one flat, square face. Then add the frame strips round the line the same way.
    • Flatten both faces again with light passes until frame and field are in one plane, and stop at 1-1/2". The board is now 12" x 13.391" (≈13-13/32").
    • Sand both faces 80, 120, 180; wipe with a damp cloth to raise the grain, let it dry, then sand 220.
    DON'Tframe the board with strips whose grain runs along the edge. The field moves across its width and length; long-grain strips do not, and the board cracks.
  12. 12

    Edges and finish

    • Ease every edge with a 1/8" chamfer and break the corners: sharp end-grain edges chip.
    • Add four rubber feet with stainless screws, inset 1" from the corners, so the board dries evenly underneath.
    • Flood with food-grade mineral oil until it stops soaking in (several coats over two days), then buff on a board butter of mineral oil and beeswax. Re-oil monthly.
